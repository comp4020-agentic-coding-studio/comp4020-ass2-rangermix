> Decide what you'll run. Or run one thing on purpose, and find out.

Choose one of the two routes below. Both carry the same weight.

## Route A: Personal Protocol Document

Your own specification: what you run, what you decline, and what you need from
other people, with the cost of each written down.

**What a strong document does.** It is specific: "I return 'how are you?' with
a token in corridors, and I don't in the morning before 10am, because it costs
me more than it buys then." It records declines as choices with prices, not as
failures. And it states needs the way week 10 taught: needs, not labels.

**What you submit.** A document in three sections, **what I run**, **what I
decline** and **what I need from others**, drawing on at least three Field
Journal entries. Length is whatever it needs; aim for 1500 to 2500 words.

**Marked on:** specificity (35%); costs recorded honestly for runs and declines
alike (30%); needs stated as needs (20%); grounding in your journal (15%).

## Route B: Neurotypical Interaction Field Experiment

Vary one parameter of the majority's protocol deliberately, record what comes
back, and report it straight.

**What a strong experiment does.** It varies **one** parameter: a longer gap
before replying, a direct request instead of a hint, not returning "how are
you?". It stays inside the range of ordinary behaviour. It records results that
went against the hypothesis as carefully as results that supported it.

**The ethics reflection is graded, not an appendix.** State what you varied,
on whom, and why it stayed inside the range of ordinary behaviour, using the
four questions from week 12's lab. The deadpan answer, that this is what the
majority does to minority implementations continuously without calling it an
experiment, is a real point. It isn't sufficient on its own.

**What you submit.** A report with hypothesis, method, results, discussion and
ethics reflection, drawing on at least three Field Journal entries. Aim for 2000
to 3000 words.

**Marked on:** design and a single controlled parameter (25%); honest reporting,
including against-hypothesis results (25%); discussion using the course's terms
(15%); ethics reflection (35%).
