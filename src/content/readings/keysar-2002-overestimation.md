Participants said ambiguous sentences while trying to convey one specific intention, then estimated whether their addressee had understood. They consistently **overestimated**: they expected to be understood more often than they actually were. The control is what makes this a mechanism rather than a mood — **overhearers who knew the speaker's intention did not systematically overestimate**. Being the one who spoke is what produces the error.

The authors' account: speakers monitoring their own utterances <span data-quoted>do not act as unbiased observers</span>. They underestimate how ambiguous their own sentence was, and overestimate how much their own disambiguating cues made the intention transparent. They close by calling this a systematic source of miscommunication in ordinary conversation.

Week 3 could be taught entirely as instructions for the person who missed the hint. This reading is why it isn't. When an envelope doesn't arrive, the sender's confidence that it was obvious is the specific thing this experiment measured and found unreliable. It is week 2's curse of expertise arriving at the level of a single sentence: fluency with your own meaning is not evidence about how it landed, and the person best placed to know is not you.
