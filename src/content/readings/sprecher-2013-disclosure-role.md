Pairs of unacquainted undergraduates completed a structured self-disclosure task in which one was randomly assigned to disclose while the other listened, and then the pair switched roles for a second interaction. After the first interaction, **listeners** reported more liking and other positive interpersonal impressions than **disclosers** did. Those differences **disappeared after the roles were swapped**. Listening was also associated with greater perceived similarity.

Week 9 had been asserting two things this study tests. First, that listening is a move rather than a pause between moves — the listeners were the ones whose liking went up, which is Collins and Miller's third effect produced experimentally rather than meta-analysed. Second, and more useful, that the course's plus-or-minus-one rule is about *both* people stepping. Here is what a one-sided exchange produces: an asymmetry in how the two people feel about it. And here is what fixes it: taking the other turn. The rate limit is not politeness. It is the mechanism by which two people end up in the same conversation.
