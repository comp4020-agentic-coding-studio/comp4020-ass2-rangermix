The actor–observer hypothesis says people explain their own behaviour by the situation and other people's by the person. Malle notes it has been <span data-quoted>described as robust, firmly established, and pervasive</span>, then reports a meta-analysis of **173 published studies** with average effect sizes from **d = −0.016 to d = 0.095**. The asymmetry held in specific conditions — a highly idiosyncratic actor, hypothetical events, actor and observer who are intimates, free-response explanations that were coded — and it held for **negative** events while **reversing for positive** ones. Across valence, the abstract concludes, no asymmetry exists.

Week 2 does not use a blanket actor–observer bias to justify the asleep test. The test is the course's own rule of thumb: a rule about an exchange can be tested in another exchange; a verdict about a person is harder to check.

The result for negative events is relevant when a journal entry records a conflict or a failed exchange. It does not mean every entry needs to concern one, and negative events were not the only condition in which the review found an asymmetry.
