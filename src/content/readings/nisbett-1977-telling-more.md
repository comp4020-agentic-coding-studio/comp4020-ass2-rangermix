The review gathers evidence for <span data-quoted>little or no direct introspective access to higher order cognitive processes</span>. People can be unaware of the thing that influenced them, unaware of their response, or unaware that the one produced the other. The paper's proposal is the part week 2 uses: when people report on their own mental processes they aren't introspecting at all. They are producing <span data-quoted>a priori, implicit causal theories</span> — judgements about whether a given thing is a *plausible* cause of a given response.

Garfinkel explains why the protocol is undocumented: it's background, and background is invisible. This explains what happens when you ask anyway. You get an answer. It is fluent, confident and constructed. That is not evasion and it is not a poor memory — the report was never introspective to begin with, so a better-motivated interviewee would produce the same thing.

The abstract's last sentence is the one to read twice, because it is the method. Reports *will* sometimes be accurate: when the influential thing is **salient** and is a **plausible** cause of the response. That tells you how to ask. "Why do people say 'how are you'?" invites the theory. "You just said 'good, you?' — what would have happened if you'd said 'terrible'?" points at something salient that happened nine seconds ago. Week 2's interview technique is that sentence turned into a rule.
