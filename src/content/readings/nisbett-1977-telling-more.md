The review gathers evidence for <span data-quoted>little or no direct introspective access to higher order cognitive processes</span>. People can be unaware of the thing that influenced them, unaware of their response, or unaware that the one produced the other. The paper proposes that these reports draw on <span data-quoted>a priori, implicit causal theories</span> — judgements about whether a given thing is a *plausible* cause of a given response.

For week 2's interviews, a fluent explanation of a convention can be a plausible theory of it. The interviewee does not have to be evasive for the account to be mistaken.

The authors also describe when reports can be accurate: when the influential thing is **salient** and is a **plausible** cause of the response. Week 2 uses that limit to propose questions about a specific exchange. "You just said 'good, you?' — what would have happened if you'd said 'terrible'?" still asks for a **prediction**, not an observation. Keep that answer as a hypothesis and test it against a real exchange; pointing at a recent event does not turn a counterfactual into something that happened.
