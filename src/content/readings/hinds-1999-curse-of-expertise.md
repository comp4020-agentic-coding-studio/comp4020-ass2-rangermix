Experts, intermediate users and novices predicted how long a complex task would take a novice; in the second study, expertise was manipulated rather than measured. In both studies, **more expertise went with worse prediction**, always in the direction of underestimating what the novice was in for. The participants were **resistant to debiasing techniques** aimed at that underestimation. Telling an expert about the bias did not repair the estimate. People with an **intermediate** level of expertise predicted more accurately than the experts did.

This is the second half of week 2's answer to "why won't they just tell us?". Garfinkel says the rule is invisible to the person running it. Nisbett and Wilson say the answer you get instead is a plausible theory. Hinds says that even where a fluent user can describe the thing, their estimate of what it costs someone who isn't fluent is unreliable — and stays unreliable after you point it out.

For week 2, try asking someone who still remembers learning the convention. This is a teaching suggestion: Hinds timed a technical task, not a conversation. A fluent speaker's “it's obvious” need not be a reliable estimate of what learning it will cost you.
