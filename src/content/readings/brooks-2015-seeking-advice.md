People hold back from asking for advice <span data-quoted>for fear of appearing incompetent</span>. The paper's next sentence is the one week 8 reads out: <span data-quoted>This fear, however, is misplaced.</span> Across their experiments, the person who asked for advice was judged **more** competent than the person who did not. Three things moved the effect: it was stronger when the task was **difficult** rather than easy, when advice was sought from that person **personally** rather than from others, and when it was sought from **experts** rather than from non-experts or not at all.

Flynn and Lake, earlier in the same week, answer one of the two fears that keep an email in drafts — *they will say no*. This answers the other, which is usually the louder one: *they will think less of me for asking.* Both estimates run the same direction, and both are wrong the same way.

The three moderators are the useful part, because they are the week's only advice about **choosing who to write to** rather than how to format the message. Ask about the part that is genuinely hard, not the part you could have looked up. Ask the person, not the room. Ask the person who actually knows. Note the setting: these are experiments on perceived competence in work and decision-making tasks, not a study of tutors, and the week says so rather than implying somebody measured a lecturer.
