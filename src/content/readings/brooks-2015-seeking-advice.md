People hold back from asking for advice <span data-quoted>for fear of appearing incompetent</span>. The paper's next sentence is the one week 8 reads out: <span data-quoted>This fear, however, is misplaced.</span> Across their experiments, the person who asked for advice was judged **more** competent than the person who did not. Three things moved the effect: it was stronger when the task was **difficult** rather than easy, when advice was sought from that person **personally** rather than from others, and when it was sought from **experts** rather than from non-experts or not at all.

Week 8 pairs this with Flynn and Lake for two worries that can keep an email in drafts: *will they say no?* and *will they think less of me for asking?* The studies examine different judgements; neither predicts the reply to a particular email.

The moderators help with **choosing who to write to**. Ask about the part that is genuinely hard, not the part you could have looked up. Ask the person, not the room. Ask the person who actually knows. These are experiments on perceived competence in work and decision-making tasks, not a study of university tutors.
