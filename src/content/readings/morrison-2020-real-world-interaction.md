Sixty-seven autistic and 58 non-autistic participants were put into pairs — both autistic, both non-autistic, or mixed — and had a five-minute unstructured conversation with someone they had never met. Then each rated the conversation and their partner. Not a video clip and not a vignette: an actual conversation, with both people's judgements recorded.

The first result is bleak and this course says so plainly. Autistic adults were rated <span data-quoted>more awkward, less attractive, and less socially warm</span> by autistic and non-autistic partners alike. The judgement travelled across both groups.

The second result is why the week exists. **Only the non-autistic adults' interest in a future interaction followed the judgement.** Autistic participants trended towards preferring other autistic partners, and reported **disclosing more about themselves** to autistic than to non-autistic partners.

So the rating and the withdrawal come apart, and only one of them is shared. And that disclosure measure is the most useful number in the week: how much a person opens up is not a fixed property of that person. It moves with who they are sitting opposite. Week 9 met the same claim from an entirely different literature, when Kluger and Malloy argued that a large share of the variance in question-asking belongs to the particular pair rather than the person. Two methods, two fields, one shape — which is the best reason this course has to prefer interoperation to conversion.
