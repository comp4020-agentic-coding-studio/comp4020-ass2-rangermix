The same videoed targets used by Sheppard and colleagues — people reacting to a distinctive greeting from an experimenter — were scored for **how readable** each one was. New groups of neurotypical perceivers then rated them. Neurotypical targets were rated more socially favourably than autistic targets, whether the details of the greeting were concealed (Study 1) or disclosed (Study 2). And the finding week 11 is built on: **readability correlated with favourability, r = .58 and r = .63, independent of target diagnosis.**

Week 2 says style is judged before substance. This week says reactions are misread rather than absent. Neither on its own explains why the misreading turns into being liked less, and this is the study that connects them: the unfavourable rating tracked how readable the person was to that perceiver, and it did so without regard to which group the person was in.

That "independent of diagnosis" is the whole argument. The judgement isn't attached to a category of person. It attaches to a property of a reading — and a reading has two parties in it, one of whom is doing the reading. Which is the double empathy problem with a correlation coefficient on it.

The authors hedge in their own abstract: perceivers *might* rate people unfavourably because they find them hard to read, <span data-quoted>though other interpretations of the correlation are also possible</span>. This is correlational and the course keeps it that way.
