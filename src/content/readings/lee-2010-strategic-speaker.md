The strategic speaker hints because they are <span data-quoted>uncertain of whether the hearer is cooperative or antagonistic</span>. The paradigm case in the abstract is deliberately unsocial, which is why it teaches so fast: bribing a policeman who may be corrupt or honest. A veiled bribe can be taken up by the corrupt one and ignored by the honest one, and the speaker survives either way. Ordinary interactions have the same payoff structure with emotional penalties instead of legal ones, whenever the request is implicitly forbidden by the kind of relationship the two people are in.

Then the part that extends week 3's own account. **Even when the hearer's willingness is known**, indirectness still buys something: higher-order deniability, which the authors describe as preempting certainty, gossip, and common knowledge of the request. A hint that both parties fully understand is still not a thing either of them said.

Read as a diagnostic rather than an explanation, this is the most useful sentence in the week. An envelope marks a place where **the sender is not sure of the answer**. Where the answer is certain and safe, the protocol tends to go direct. So the question to ask of an ambiguous line is not "why are they being like this" but "what are they not sure of?" — and the answer is usually visible.
