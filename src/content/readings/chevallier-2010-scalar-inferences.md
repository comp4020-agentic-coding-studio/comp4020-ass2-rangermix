A scalar inference is the step from "John or Mary will come" to "not both of them" — an enrichment the words themselves don't require, since "or" is compatible with both arriving. It is one of the most studied inferences in linguistic pragmatics, which is what makes it a fair test case.

The authors state their prediction in the abstract: because of <span data-quoted>the reported pragmatic deficits</span>, autistic participants would produce fewer of these enrichments than matched controls. What they report next is <span data-quoted>contrary to expectations</span> — performance did not differ between the groups, in line with Pijnacker and colleagues (2009).

Week 3 teaches a decoder, and a student could reasonably ask whether the decoder is the thing they are missing. For this inference, in this experiment, the answer was no, and the people who ran it had bet the other way. Hold it at that width: one inference type, one study, two matched groups. It is not a general verdict on pragmatics and the week doesn't offer it as one. What it does do is put the question where week 2's double empathy problem already put it — a mismatch has two sides, and the side that assumes it sent a clear signal has not been tested nearly as often.
