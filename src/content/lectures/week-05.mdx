"Meet you Thursday."

"This Thursday?"

"Next Thursday."

Three turns, one misunderstanding, fixed before anyone noticed there was a
problem. That exchange is this week's subject. It is the protocol's error
handling, and it is running constantly.

## Repair is infrastructure

Conversation has built-in machinery for trouble with speaking, hearing and
understanding. Schegloff, Jefferson and Sacks called it the **organisation of
repair**, and found a strong skew within it: speakers fix their own trouble far
more often than anyone else fixes it for them (Schegloff et al., 1977).

So the protocol gives the speaker the first chance. "Meet you Thursday, I mean
next Thursday" happens before a listener has to say anything.

How often does the listener have to say something? Dingemanse and colleagues
went through 48.5 hours of conversation in twelve languages from eight
language families. They found 2053 cases of a listener signalling trouble: **one
about every 1.4 minutes**, in every language sampled (Dingemanse et al.,
2015).

<MarginNote label="Uptime">
  If your conversations have gone ninety seconds without a repair, the logs
  are not complete.
</MarginNote>

Misunderstanding isn't an exception condition. It's steady-state traffic.

## Three ways to say you didn't get it

The same study found three kinds of **repair initiator**, the move a listener
makes to flag trouble. Every language sampled had all three (Dingemanse et
al., 2015):

| Type | What it does | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Open request | Flags trouble without saying where. | "Huh?" / "Sorry, what?" |
| Restricted request | Asks about one specific part. | "Who?" / "Which Thursday?" |
| Restricted offer | Offers a guess and asks for confirmation. | "She had a boy?" / "This Thursday?" |

And the rule that decides between them: **people choose the most specific
initiator available**. It costs both sides least. A restricted offer
("This Thursday?") means the speaker only has to say yes, or give one word. An
open request means they have to repeat everything.

That is the week's first sincere instruction. When you've half-understood,
**say the part you got**. "Sorry, you want the draft *by* Friday, or *on*
Friday?" repairs faster than "what?", and it shows the speaker exactly which
part you did catch.

The word *faster* there is literal. Kendrick timed 169 other-initiations of
repair: the ones that **located the trouble specifically** arrived after
**shorter gaps** than the ones that didn't, and the rare ones that simply
corrected an error arrived **without delay** (Kendrick, 2015). Most repair
initiations came after gaps of about **700 ms** --- so the move that flags
trouble is itself one of the slow turns, which is worth knowing if you have
ever felt you were too late to ask.

And part of what that delay is doing, on Kendrick's analysis, is **giving the
first speaker a chance to fix it themselves**. Silence before a repair is not
only hesitation. Sometimes it is room.

## "Huh?" is a word

When you haven't understood any of it, the open request is correct. It is a
real word, not a failure to produce one. Dingemanse, Torreira and Enfield
found "huh?" in much the same form and function across ten languages, far
more alike than chance would explain. It is learnt, not an involuntary noise,
and it exists because every language needs a way to say "I didn't get that"
(Dingemanse et al., 2013).

If "sorry, what?" makes you wince, you are embarrassed by a universal feature
of human language. The "sorry" is optional.

The step people skip is the last one. A repair is accepted in **one turn**
("got it", "ah, next Thursday") and the conversation carries on. Apologising
for the misunderstanding, from either side, turns a one-turn repair into a
three-turn one, and moves the conversation's attention from Thursday to the
two of you.

## When the error is an offence

Mishearing a day is cheap. Letting someone down isn't, and the protocol
switches to a different subroutine: the apology.

Apologies have parts. Lewicki, Polin and Lount tested six components drawn
from earlier research, alone and combined. **Apologies with more components
were rated more effective**, and some components counted for more than others.
Apologies for failures of competence ("I got the time wrong") also landed
better than apologies for failures of integrity ("I lied about it") (Lewicki et
al., 2016).

We read their abstract, not the full paper, so the course won't claim which
component matters most.

Kirchhoff, Wagner and Strack found the same direction in two experimental
vignette studies, and added a mechanism. More verbal elements increased
forgiveness, **especially after a more severe offence**, and the link between
an apology's completeness and forgiveness was **mediated by anger reduction**
(Kirchhoff et al., 2012). So a fuller apology isn't a performance of
sincerity. It is doing a job: it brings the anger down, and forgiveness
follows that.

For a severe offence, Study 2 found four elements particularly relevant:
**conveying emotions**, **admitting fault**, **a statement of apology** such as
"I apologize", and **an attempt at explanation**.

### The template, corrected

That last element caught this course out. Earlier versions of this page told
you to keep explanation out of an apology. That was too strong, and here is the
revision, with what changed marked.

| Part | Sounds like |
| --- | --- |
| **Say it** | "I'm sorry." |
| Name what happened | "I didn't send my section before the meeting." |
| Say it was yours | "That's on me, not the deadline." |
| Say what it cost them | "You had to present without it." |
| **Explain, briefly** | "I'd misread the day." |
| Say what happens now | "It's in the doc now, and I'll send the next one a day early." |
| Offer a fix | "Want me to take the Q&A next time?" |

Two rows are new. **"I'm sorry"** was missing entirely, which is faintly
absurd for an apology template, and Study 2 lists a statement of apology among
the elements that mattered. **The explanation** is now in, with two
constraints that are the course's own and were the real objection all along:
it goes **after** the ownership, never before, and it stays **shorter than the
apology**. An explanation that arrives first, or runs long, is heard as an
excuse --- which is a claim about how it lands, not about honesty.

The one thing the template still refuses is **"but"**. Everything before it
gets deleted by everything after it.

The part that's easiest to leave out is **what it cost them**. Without it, an
apology is about you; with it, the other person knows you saw what happened
from their side. That row is the course's, not a finding.

A caveat worth saying out loud: Kirchhoff et al. is two vignette studies in
Germany. The direction is well supported; the exact four are not a universal
parts list.

### Repair is not a personality

Fehr, Gelfand and Nag meta-analysed **175 studies and 26,006 participants** on
whether one person forgives another. Apology came out at ***r* = .42**, and
state anger at ***r* = -.41**, which sits neatly beside Kirchhoff's
anger-reduction route from a completely different direction (Fehr et al.,
2010).

The result this week leans on hardest is the next one. **Situational
constructs accounted for greater variance in forgiveness than victims'
dispositions.** What you do and say after an error matters more than who the
other person happens to be.

Two folk theories also die in that abstract: the effect of **gender was
non-significant** (*r* = .01) and the effect of **age was negligible**
(*r* = .06).

<MarginNote label="Closing the loop">
  The situation beats the disposition. Which is to say: it's a protocol.
</MarginNote>

This is the evidence for the claim at the bottom of this page, which earlier
versions asserted and could not support. Nobody has to be a naturally
forgiving person, or a naturally graceful apologiser. The parts are the parts,
and they work on the anger rather than on your character.

## Receiving repair

Both subroutines have a receiving side, and it is just as undocumented.

- Someone corrects a misunderstanding of yours: **update and carry on**. "Oh,
  next Thursday. Great." No apology needed; nothing went wrong.
- Someone apologises to you: **accept in one turn**, if you can mean it.
  "Thanks for saying that. We're fine." If you can't mean it yet, "Thanks. I
  need a bit of time" is a conforming reply.

The subroutine this course is named for, in a way, is the one nobody documents
because everyone assumes it's a matter of character. It isn't. It's error
handling, with a spec, and now you have it.
