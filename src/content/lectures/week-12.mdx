Standards documents end with a section called something like *Open Issues*:
the problems the authors know about, have decided not to solve in this
version, and would like the next reader to take on. This course ends the same
way.

Three parts of the protocol are left out of scope. Each gets an issue number,
a reason, the reading where its documentation starts, and a question you
could take into your capstone.

## Why these three are out of scope

Each one runs on top of everything the course has documented: handshakes,
envelopes, timing, repair, masking, disclosure and rate limits, all at once.
Each one is high-stakes enough that a first-year course would teach it badly
in one week. And in each one, running the protocol *on purpose* raises
questions the course isn't equipped to settle for you.

### OI-1: Romance

**Status:** open. **Why out of scope:** every earlier week at once, with the
highest cost of error.

**Where the documentation starts.** Hall and colleagues identified five styles
of communicating romantic interest (traditional, physical, sincere, playful and
polite) from over five thousand adults. The physical, sincere and playful
styles correlated with more dating success (Hall et al., 2010). The inventory
measures how people say they signal interest, not what receivers understand.

**The course's reading of it.** If there are five styles, then "I can't tell
when someone's flirting" is sometimes a style mismatch rather than a failure to
read. Week 3's decoder would handle the sincere style well and the playful
style badly. That's the course's inference, not the paper's finding.

**Asked of the people concerned.** The inventory above is general-population
and self-reported, so on its own it would leave this issue resting on how
people say they flirt. Sala, Hooley and Stokes asked 31 autistic and 26
non-autistic people what makes emotional and physical intimacy possible, and
what blocks it (Sala et al., 2020).

Both groups named the same enablers: **communication**, **sharing and
similarity**, **respect and safety for self and other**, and **working on the
relationship**. Both named conflict, within a person and between people, as a
barrier. What the authors report as specific to the autistic participants is
**uncertainty** --- about relationships, and about communication. Their
conclusion is that the two groups hold similar notions of intimacy and meet
different challenges in reaching it.

<MarginNote label="No new machinery">
  Same destination. Different obstacle.
</MarginNote>

**Uncertainty** points back to week 3's envelope --- where the sender doesn't
know the answer --- and week 9's rate limit, for moving when you can't predict
the other person's next step. Those give you two places to start with OI-1.

The paper's opening line states the clinical framing its authors are writing
inside. This course takes the findings and leaves the framing, which is the
same call week 2 made about the thin-slice study.

**A capstone question.** For a Personal Protocol Document: what are your rate
limit (week 9) and your need-without-label sentences (week 10) for dating?

### OI-2: Persuasion

**Status:** open. **Why out of scope:** the protocol used deliberately to move
someone, where the ethics are the hard part.

**Where the documentation starts.** Cialdini and Goldstein's review organises
the research on compliance and conformity around three goals people already
pursue: seeing things **accurately**, keeping their **relationships**, and
feeling good about **themselves**. Much of the influence it describes is
subtle, indirect, and outside the target's awareness (Cialdini & Goldstein,
2004).

<MarginNote label="Security note">
  A protocol that works outside awareness is, by definition, an attack surface.
</MarginNote>

**The course's reading of it.** The decoder reads an envelope; it also writes
one. The rate limit paces closeness; it can also pace a sale.

**And the reason the reflection carries marks.** Route B of the capstone
varies a protocol parameter in exchanges with other people, who may not have
agreed to be part of a study. The
ethics reflection is worth 35%: you must account for what you do to them.

Thursday's lab reviews every proposed parameter against four questions:

1. **Is it ordinary here?** Is this something people do in this setting?
2. **Who is it on?** And why is that group the appropriate one?
3. **What could it cost them?** Not "is it harmless" --- what, specifically,
   and how would you notice?
4. **What stops it?** At what point do you stop varying and repair, and what
   is the repair?

Garfinkel's method deliberately breached expectancies. This experiment keeps
variations inside ordinary behaviour in the setting at hand. There is no
universal minimum amount of eye contact. Someone noticing a change gives you
information for questions 3 and 4; it is not an automatic failure or a reason
to conceal the experiment. Do not turn another person's discomfort into a
result you must obtain before you are allowed to stop.

### One parameter, two contexts

Dev's fictional proposal replaces a hint about noise with a direct request:
"Could we work at the quieter table?"

| Question | A possible answer for Dev's study session |
| --- | --- |
| Ordinary here? | Two classmates are already deciding where to sit; either can suggest a table. |
| Who is affected? | The classmate Dev arranged to study with, not strangers recruited for a reaction. |
| Possible cost? | Moving books, interrupting a task, or feeling pressed to agree. Watch for reluctance and allow a no. |
| Stop and repair? | Ask once. At a refusal or reluctance, stop pursuing the variation and agree where to work without collecting further reactions. |

Now change the context: the classmate has already started a timed online test.
The same request can impose a different cost. Postpone the experiment; the
sentence's wording did not give it a universal permit.

### In the room --- check your parameter (7 min)

**One minute, alone.** Write your proposed parameter, or use Dev's fictional
one. State the setting as well as the behaviour.

**Four minutes, in pairs.** Two minutes per proposal: one asks all four
questions and the other answers briefly, then swap. Route A students can
review the fictional proposal.

**Two minutes.** Record a possible cost, a stopping condition, and any answer
you still need before Thursday's fuller review.

**A capstone question.** For a Field Experiment: can you vary one parameter
without trying to change what anybody decides?

### OI-3: Power

**Status:** open, and contested. **Why out of scope:** it changes every other
parameter, and the research is still arguing.

**Where the documentation starts.** Keltner, Gruenfeld and Anderson proposed
that elevated power activates **approach**: more positive affect, more
automatic processing, more disinhibited behaviour. Reduced power activates
**inhibition**: more attention to threat and to **other people's interests**,
more controlled processing, more inhibited behaviour (Keltner et al., 2003).
The account has drawn published criticism, so treat it as a proposal, not a
settled result.

**The criticism.** Moskowitz published a comment in the same journal a year
later. The objection is **not** that the proposal is false. It is that the
studies behind it <span data-quoted>have permitted examining the opposite
effects but not the specific effects of high and low power</span> --- they can
show high and low power differing, but not that each half of the proposal does
what it separately claims. The comment then sets out designs and analytic
strategies that would let researchers evaluate the specific claims (Moskowitz,
2004).

The comment gives us a format for an erratum: name the claim, say what the
evidence cannot distinguish, and specify the test that would close it.

<MarginNote label="The standard">
  An erratum that names the test it would take to close it. Measure this
  course's own against that.
</MarginNote>

There is a further update --- the authors revisited the theory years later ---
and this course could not retrieve an abstract for it, so it isn't cited here.
That gap is itself part of the open issue: OI-3 cannot currently tell you how
the argument came out.

**The course's reading of it.** If the proposal holds even partly, then
**whoever holds less power runs more of the protocol**. They attend more to
the other person, inhibit more, and pay week 6's costs more often. Week 11's
finding that conversion falls on the minority is the same shape. The connection
is the course's; the proposal is theirs.

**A capstone question.** For either route: in which of your relationships do
you run the most protocol, and who holds the power in it?

## Errata

A specification that publishes no known issues is either very new or not being
honest. This course's are listed below. Each can guide a question for your
journal or capstone; your own observations will not settle every general claim.

Hold them to Moskowitz's standard: a good known issue names the claim, says
what the evidence can't currently distinguish, and specifies the test that
would address it. Some of the five below do that better than others. Today's
workshop makes the missing parts explicit.

### A worked erratum

"The email rules don't work" is a complaint without a test. Try this instead:

- **Model:** week 8's one-request default makes it easier to see whether a
  reply answered the request.
- **Evidence limit:** the course has not compared that default with clearly
  numbered requests in one email. The cited register study does not isolate
  this clause.
- **Small test:** give consenting classmates fictional exchanges with the same
  three requests, either separated or bundled. Vary which format comes first.
  After a partial reply, ask them to mark unanswered requests; compare missed
  requests and time taken. No real email needs sending.

That test could challenge the classroom rule. It would not establish how every
tutor handles a real inbox. Put that limit beside the result too.

### In the room --- write E-6 (12 min)

**Two minutes, in pairs.** Choose one course-model claim below, or another
that is not E-1 to E-5. Read the worked example before drafting.

- Week 8: one request per email helps a reader identify what remains unanswered.
- Week 8: agreeing a going-quiet message reduces guesses about a group member's silence.
- Week 11: shared course vocabulary helps partners specify a mismatch.

**Five minutes.** Write the **model**, what the current evidence **cannot
distinguish**, and a **test**. Name an observable result that would count against
your model. Use fictional material and willing participants where possible.

**Three minutes.** Swap with a nearby pair. Can a person run the test this
year without a grant? Does it test the stated claim? What would remain unknown?

**Two minutes.** Revise one weak part. At most two volunteers read an erratum
to the room; everyone keeps their own version for the capstone.

### The errata you should trust least

Pay particular attention to E-3 and E-2 when you use them in your capstone.

E-3, the depth scale, is used in week 9 to teach a rule about pacing, drawn as
a ladder, and given a spec line. It is five levels somebody invented. If depth
isn't ordered that way, the numerical ±1 rule needs revision or abandonment.
The ability to ask, hold or decline does not depend on the ladder being valid.

E-2, recovery hours, is the unit of week 6's ledger. The course has not
established a measured price. Keep the observations and compare your estimates
with what the following days were actually like. Revise the planning rule if
it does not help. Consistency alone does not guarantee a useful ranking of
weeks, and the estimates should not be reported as measured masking costs.

## What this course refused to teach you

There is a version of this material that ends with *pass as one of them*.
That is not this course's outcome.

A convention is not a condition. What you were handed is the specification of
one, and a convention is a thing you can read, run, and decline.

The joke was never at anyone in this room. It was at a protocol that nobody
wrote down and that everybody insists is self-evident.

## What the course documented

Twelve weeks, four parts:

- **Read** the protocol: the handshake, why nobody wrote it down, envelopes,
  and the clock.
- **Implement** selectively: repair, masking as a budget, and a runbook for
  when the budget runs out.
- **Negotiate** with it: institutions, closeness, disclosure, and other
  implementations.
- **Horizon**: this page.

The outcome was never that you become indistinguishable from anyone. It was
fluency plus choice: read the protocol, run a part of it when it's worth the
cost, and decline a part of it knowing what declining costs.

## The capstone

Due **Sunday 13 June, 23:59**, two weeks after teaching ends. Two routes,
equal weight:

- **Personal Protocol Document.** What you run, what you decline, and what you
  need from others, with the costs attached.
- **Neurotypical Interaction Field Experiment.** Vary one protocol parameter
  deliberately, record what comes back, and report it straight, with a graded
  ethics reflection.

Thursday's lab is the clinic for both.

### A Route A example

This is an invented practice document, not journal evidence for a submission:

- **What I run:** "I check the group chat at 18:00. I usually budget ten
  minutes; my notes would need to show whether that estimate holds."
- **What I decline:** "I skip the optional coffee after the lab to catch my
  bus. I miss that time with the group; I do not yet know whether it changes
  how often they invite me."
- **What I need from others:** "Put the task and its deadline in the shared
  document, so I can check it after my shift."

If no decline is affordable in your circumstances, say so under the second
heading. You can compare a hypothetical decline with what you currently run,
keeping predicted consequences separate from observed costs. A compulsory
refusal would be a strange assessment of choice.

### In the room --- pick your route (9 min)

Use these nine minutes to prepare a first paragraph for Thursday's clinic.

**One minute, alone.** Choose Route A or Route B.

**Five minutes, alone.** Write a first paragraph. For Route A, start under one
of its three headings and leave a line for each of the other two. State a cost
you know or a prediction you would need to check. "No affordable decline now"
is allowed. For Route B, name one parameter and setting, then draft brief
answers to all four ethics questions.

**Three minutes, in pairs.** Take turns checking the structure; private details
can stay private. For Route A, are all three headings represented, with
observations separate from predictions? For Route B, are all four contextual
questions answered, including a stopping condition? Mark one gap for Thursday.

### One thing before you go

The register above leaves three open issues and five published errata, plus
whatever the room filed today.

The protocol you have been studying has none of either. No issue numbers, no
errata, no version, no comment published beside it in the same journal a year
later. It is not maintained. It is not documented. And the people who run it
fluently will tell you, with complete sincerity, that it is obvious.

You now have twelve weeks of evidence that it is not, and the vocabulary to say
so precisely. What you do with that is the part this course was never going to
decide for you.
