You've had three good conversations with someone from your lab. Really good
ones. And yet it isn't a friendship, and you can't tell what's missing.

Or: someone you've met twice told you about their parents' divorce in the café
queue, and you didn't know what to say back.

Both are the same undocumented system, running at different speeds. This week
is about its pace.

## Friendship costs hours

Hall asked adults who had recently moved about a new acquaintance, and followed
first-year students over nine weeks. In both studies, **hours spent together were associated with
becoming closer**. Time spent on leisure activities predicted closeness, and so
did everyday talk (Hall, 2019).

We couldn't read the full paper, only its abstract, so this page quotes no
hour counts from it. The finding that matters doesn't need any: **the protocol
expects hours before depth**. Three great conversations are three hours, not a
friendship yet. That isn't a verdict on the conversations. It's the clock.

<MarginNote label="Capacity planning">
  Friendship is provisioned in hours. There is no way to buy it in one large
  conversation.
</MarginNote>

## Disclosure is the engine

Hours are the clock. **Disclosure** is the engine. Collins and Miller combined
the research on self-disclosure and liking, and found three effects, all
significant (Collins & Miller, 1994):

1. People who make more intimate disclosures tend to be liked more.
2. People disclose more to those they already like.
3. People come to like those they have disclosed *to*.

The third is the easiest to miss. Receiving someone's disclosure well builds
closeness too, on their side. Listening is a move, not a pause between moves.

The effects vary with the kind of disclosure and the setting. "Disclose more,
get liked more" is not a rule you can run in a café queue.

## Escalation works in steps

Aron and colleagues found a way to generate closeness in a lab. Pairs of
strangers spent 45 minutes on self-disclosure tasks that **gradually escalated
in intensity**, and ended up closer than pairs who spent the time on small
talk. Making "get close" an explicit goal made no significant difference.
Neither did matching people on attitudes (Aron et al., 1997).

The word to take from that study is *gradually*. The procedure moves both
people up together, a step at a time. It is not evidence that jumping
straight to depth works outside a lab, where nobody has agreed to the steps.

## The rate limit

Here is the course's model of the undocumented pace. The levels and the rule
are ours, not the literature's; test them in your journal.

| Level | Depth | Sounds like |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 0 | logistics, weather, the course | "Busy week?" |
| 1 | low-stakes preferences and opinions | "I can't stand that café." |
| 2 | experiences, and small difficulties | "The stats quiz went badly." |
| 3 | ongoing struggles, values, family | "Things are hard at home at the moment." |
| 4 | fears, health, things few people know | "I've been having panic attacks." |

**The rule:** match the other person's last level, plus or minus one. Step up
one level at a time, and wait for them to step before you step again.

That's why the café-queue divorce landed strangely. It went from level 0 to
level 3 in one turn, with no step from the listener in between. And it's why
three conversations at level 1 don't become a friendship. Nobody stepped.

### Signals you've gone past a rate limit

The course's working list, for testing in your journal:

- the other person's replies get shorter;
- the topic comes back to level 0 ("anyway, did you do the reading?");
- a laugh or a joke where a response to the content would go;
- they physically start to leave.

### The step-down

Week 5's repair, applied to depth. Name it in one turn and go down a level:

> "Sorry, that was a lot for a Tuesday. How was your weekend?"

The step-down tells the other person you noticed, and that they don't have to
match level 3 in a queue.

## Choosing your own rate

The rate limit isn't a speed you're obliged to reach. You can keep someone at
level 1 for a whole semester, on purpose. Some people run the protocol with a
high limit and some with a low one, and neither is the correct setting.

Declining escalation costs something, and it belongs in your week 6 ledger:
the other person may read a steady level 1 as distance. If you want them to
read it differently, say so, once: "I'm slow to talk about personal stuff, but
I like these conversations." That's a hold token for depth.
