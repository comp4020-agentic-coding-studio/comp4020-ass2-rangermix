You've had three good conversations with someone from your lab. Really good
ones. And yet it isn't a friendship, and you can't tell what's missing.

Or: someone you've met twice told you about their parents' divorce in the café
queue, and you didn't know what to say back.

Both are the same undocumented system, running at different speeds. This week
is about its pace.

## Friendship costs hours

Hall asked adults who had recently moved about a new acquaintance, and followed
first-year students over nine weeks. In both studies, **hours spent together were associated with
becoming closer**. Time spent on leisure activities predicted closeness, and so
did everyday talk (Hall, 2019).

We couldn't read the full paper, only its abstract, so this page quotes no
hour counts from it. The finding that matters doesn't need any: **the protocol
expects hours before depth**. Three great conversations are three hours, not a
friendship yet. That isn't a verdict on the conversations. It's the clock.

<MarginNote label="Capacity planning">
  Friendship is provisioned in hours. There is no way to buy it in one large
  conversation.
</MarginNote>

## Disclosure is the engine

Hours are the clock. **Disclosure** is the engine. Collins and Miller combined
the research on self-disclosure and liking, and found three effects, all
significant (Collins & Miller, 1994):

1. People who make more intimate disclosures tend to be liked more.
2. People disclose more to those they already like.
3. People come to like those they have disclosed *to*.

The third is the easiest to miss. Receiving someone's disclosure well builds
closeness too, on their side. Listening is a move, not a pause between moves.

The effects vary with the kind of disclosure and the setting. "Disclose more,
get liked more" is not a rule you can run in a café queue.

### Which side gains, and for how long

Sprecher, Treger and Wondra ran that third effect as an experiment. Pairs of
**unacquainted undergraduates** did a structured disclosure task. One was
randomly assigned to **disclose**; the other to **listen**. Then they swapped
and did it again (Sprecher et al., 2013).

After the first interaction, the **listeners** reported more liking and other
positive impressions than the **disclosers** did. Listening was also associated
with greater **perceived similarity**.

Then the sentence this whole week rests on:

> Those differences **disappeared after participants switched roles.**

<MarginNote label="Handshake, again">
  One-sided exchange, asymmetric result. Both sides step, and it resolves.
</MarginNote>

Read that as a protocol result. A one-sided exchange doesn't fail --- it leaves
the two people **feeling differently about the same conversation**. What fixes
it is not saying more, or saying less. It is **taking the other turn.**

That is the evidence under the rule in §4, which until now this course
justified by reasoning about cost. The rate limit isn't politeness. It is the
mechanism by which two people end up in the same conversation.

The scope is narrow and worth saying: strangers, a structured task, two
interactions. It tells you nothing about how depth paces over months.

## Escalation works in steps

Aron and colleagues found a way to generate closeness in a lab. Pairs of
strangers spent 45 minutes on self-disclosure tasks that **gradually escalated
in intensity**, and ended up closer than pairs who spent the time on small
talk. Making "get close" an explicit goal made no significant difference.
Neither did matching people on attitudes (Aron et al., 1997).

The word to take from that study is *gradually*. The procedure moves both
people up together, a step at a time. It is not evidence that jumping
straight to depth works outside a lab, where nobody has agreed to the steps.

## The rate limit

Here is the course's model of the undocumented pace. The levels and the rule
are ours, not the literature's; test them in your journal.

| Level | Depth | Sounds like |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 0 | logistics, weather, the course | "Busy week?" |
| 1 | low-stakes preferences and opinions | "I can't stand that café." |
| 2 | experiences, and small difficulties | "The stats quiz went badly." |
| 3 | ongoing struggles, values, family | "Things are hard at home at the moment." |
| 4 | fears, health, things few people know | "I've been having panic attacks." |

**The rule:** match the other person's last level, plus or minus one. Step up
one level at a time, and wait for them to step before you step again.

That's why the café-queue divorce landed strangely. It went from level 0 to
level 3 in one turn, with no step from the listener in between. And it's why
three conversations at level 1 don't become a friendship. Nobody stepped.

### Signals you've gone past a rate limit

The course's working list, for testing in your journal:

- the other person's replies get shorter;
- the topic comes back to level 0 ("anyway, did you do the reading?");
- a laugh or a joke where a response to the content would go;
- they physically start to leave.

### In the room --- one turn each (9 min)

In pairs. Four minutes: **A** answers a level-2 question from the board while
**B** only listens --- no disclosing, no matching, questions allowed.

Then swap, and four minutes the other way.

One minute at the end, both answer privately: *after the first half, which of
you felt warmer towards the other?* Then: *did that change after the swap?*

Sprecher et al. found the listener warmer, and the difference gone after the
switch. You are not replicating a study with a class exercise --- you are
finding out whether the shape is recognisable from inside it.

### The step-down

Week 5's repair, applied to depth. Name it in one turn and go down a level:

> "Sorry, that was a lot for a Tuesday. How was your weekend?"

The step-down tells the other person you noticed, and that they don't have to
match level 3 in a queue.

## Did it land?

The ladder has rungs and no receipt. It tells you how far a turn went; it says
nothing about whether the person you said it to came away feeling heard. That
turns out to be where most of the work is.

Laurenceau, Feldman Barrett and Pietromonaco had people fill in a record
**immediately after every social interaction** --- one week in the first study,
two in the second. For each one: what you disclosed, what they disclosed, how
responsive they seemed, and how intimate it felt (Laurenceau et al., 1998).

<MarginNote label="You are doing this">
  An event-contingent diary. Filled in after the interaction, not at the end of
  the week. Your Field Journal is this method.
</MarginNote>

Intimacy in a single interaction came out as **both people's disclosure**, with
**perceived partner responsiveness** as a partial mediator. Not what was said.
Whether the person who said it thought they had been received.

So the rate limit gains a second question. Not just *was that within one level*,
but *did they give me anything back*.

### Emotion outranked facts

The detail that changes the advice is in their second study. Disclosing
**emotion** predicted intimacy better than disclosing **facts and information**.

Read that against the ladder and something useful falls out. A step up does not
have to mean a more private subject. It can be the **same subject with the
feeling attached**:

| Facts | The same thing, with the emotion |
| --- | --- |
| "The stats quiz was 40% of the mark." | "The stats quiz was 40%, and I've been sick about it since Tuesday." |
| "I moved here in February." | "I moved here in February and I still don't know anyone." |
| "My flatmate moved out." | "My flatmate moved out, and the place is very quiet." |

The left column is level 2 by subject and lands like level 0. The right column
is the same information, one rung higher, at no extra cost in privacy. If you
have ever been told you give people information and not much else, that is the
column to practise --- and notice that the fix is *additive*. Nothing in the
left column was wrong.

## The move nobody expects to work

There is a documented, cheap, repeatable way to be liked more in a
conversation, and the people who benefit from it do not know they are.

Huang and colleagues studied three sets of live conversations between pairs:
two online get-acquainted conversations and a field study of face-to-face speed
dating. People who asked **more questions, and follow-up questions in
particular**, were liked more by the person they were talking to. People
instructed to ask more questions were rated higher in
<span data-quoted>responsiveness</span> --- the construct the paper glosses as
listening, understanding, validation and care --- and responsiveness is what
explained the effect (Huang et al., 2017).

A **follow-up question** picks up what the other person just said. A switch
question starts a new topic. Both are questions; they are not the same move:

> **Them:** "I've been trying to get the stats assignment finished all weekend."
>
> *Switch:* "Have you chosen your elective yet?"
>
> *Follow-up:* "Which part of it is taking the time?"

The follow-up is the cheapest turn in this entire week. It costs you no
disclosure at all. It is a way to take your turn --- which §4 says you have to
do --- **without** stepping up a level, which is exactly what you want when the
other person has just gone to level 3 in a café queue and you are not going
there with them.

Then the last line of the abstract:

> people do not anticipate that question-asking increases interpersonal liking.

<MarginNote label="Undocumented, again">
  An effective move, available to everyone, that its own users don't know they
  are running. This course in one sentence.
</MarginNote>

### In the room --- the follow-up (8 min)

Three minutes, on your own. Take a sentence someone said to you this week ---
any sentence, dull is fine --- and write **three** follow-up questions to it.
Not three questions. Three questions that could only have been asked of *that
sentence*.

Three minutes in pairs: read yours out. Your partner says which words in the
original each one picks up. If they can't find the words, it's a switch
question wearing a follow-up's clothes.

Two minutes, the whole room: the hard ones. Which sentences were hard to
follow up, and what were they missing?

### The part where the research argues with itself

Here is something the social protocol has never had, and this paper does.

In **2025** the journal published a correction to it. An independent audit
found <span data-quoted>several minor errors in how some results were
reported</span>, and stated that all the conclusions in the paper are valid,
that the substantive result of every hypothesis test is unchanged, and that the
corrected results and the audit materials are posted publicly.

In **2019** Kluger and Malloy published a reanalysis of the speed-dating study.
Their argument: being offered a second date is not the same thing as liking;
follow-up and switch questions weren't distinguishable as latent scores; and
about a third of the variance in how much someone asks questions belongs to
**the particular pair**, not to the person. Huang's group published a reply
saying the reanalysis tested the wrong variables.

This course is not going to adjudicate a social relations model it hasn't run.
It takes three things from the argument, and you should too:

1. the effect in the two lab conversations, which the reanalysis doesn't touch;
2. **a second date is a proxy for liking, not liking** --- worth holding onto,
   because the depth scale above is also a proxy;
3. the dyad point, as a claim you can test: how much you ask questions may
   depend less on you than on who you are with.

And then notice the shape of all that. A numbered correction. A signed
objection. A reply. Anyone can read the lot and decide.

Nobody has ever issued an erratum for "how are you".

## Choosing your own rate

The rate limit isn't a speed you're obliged to reach. You can keep someone at
level 1 for a whole semester, on purpose. Some people run the protocol with a
high limit and some with a low one, and neither is the correct setting.

Declining escalation costs something, and it belongs in your week 6 ledger:
the other person may read a steady level 1 as distance. If you want them to
read it differently, say so, once: "I'm slow to talk about personal stuff, but
I like these conversations." That's a hold token for depth.
