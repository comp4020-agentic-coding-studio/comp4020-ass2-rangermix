Slop University publishes a forty-page assessment policy, a thirty-page
academic integrity procedure, and a flowchart for appealing a mark. It
publishes nothing about how to ask a tutor a question without sounding rude,
or how to ask for an extension without sounding like an excuse.

Welcome back from the break. Part III of the course is about negotiating with
the protocol, and it starts with the most formal version of it: institutions.

## The format has a price

Institutional messages run on an undocumented register, and getting it wrong
has a measured cost. Stephens, Houser and Cowan showed instructors student
emails written either casually or formally. The difference accounted for
**between 48% and 64% of the variance** in how the instructors felt about the
student, how credible they found them, and their attitude to the message. It
also affected whether they agreed to a simple request to meet (Stephens et al.,
2009).

The same study asked instructors and students what bothered them. Instructors
were bothered more, especially by **unsigned emails** and **shortcuts like
"RU"**. Students put the problem down to technology; instructors put it down
to training. That is what an undocumented expectation looks like: two groups
following different rules, each assuming theirs is the obvious one.

It is 2009 data from the US, and email norms drift. The course treats it as
evidence that the register matters, not as a ruling on emoji.

## Ask directly

The other half of institutional protocol is whether you ask at all. People
underestimate how likely others are to say yes to a direct request for help,
by **as much as 50%**. The reason: someone deciding whether to ask thinks about
what saying yes costs the helper, and forgets that saying no is socially costly
too (Flynn & Lake, 2008).

<MarginNote label="Probability estimate">
  Your estimate of the odds is wrong, and wrong in your disfavour.
</MarginNote>

A clear, answerable request is the easiest kind of request to say yes to.

## The friction is at the interface

Compared with non-autistic students, autistic students at UK universities
reported more challenges with the social side of university life, and also
reported academic strengths (Gurbuz et al., 2019). The shape of that finding is
the week's premise. The work is often not the hard part. The hard part is the
undocumented layer around it: the email, the office door, the group chat.

Everyone meets that layer. Some people arrived already fluent in it, from
family or school. Nobody was handed it in writing. So here it is.

## Specimen: the email to a tutor

The course's specification, in seven clauses. The wording is a template; the
clauses are what a tutor's inbox rewards.

> **Subject:** [NT101] Protocol Specification: can my subroutine be a group chat one?
>
> Hi Jonah,
>
> I'm in the Thursday lab. For the Protocol Specification, I'd like to document
> how a group chat decides on a meeting time.
>
> Is a subroutine that runs in text, not speech, in scope?
>
> I've checked the brief, and it doesn't say either way.
>
> I'm planning to start the draft on Monday, so a reply before then would help.
>
> Thanks,
> Alex Chen (u7654321)

| # | Clause | In the specimen |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | The subject line carries the course code and the request. | "[NT101] Protocol Specification: can my…" |
| 2 | Greet by name. | "Hi Jonah," |
| 3 | Give context in one or two sentences, no more. | the Thursday lab, and the topic |
| 4 | Make **one** request, as a question with an answerable shape. | "Is a subroutine that runs in text in scope?" |
| 5 | Say what you've already checked. | "I've checked the brief" |
| 6 | Say when you need it by, and why. | "before Monday" |
| 7 | Sign off with your full name and student number. | "Alex Chen (u7654321)" |

Clause 4 does the most work. An email with three requests gets the easiest one
answered. Send three emails.

## Specimen: the extension request

Ask **before** the deadline, and ask for **a date**. The request is the date;
the reason is supporting information, and it can be short.

> **Subject:** [NT101] Extension request: Interoperation Report
>
> Hi Wren,
>
> I'd like to request an extension on the Interoperation Report to Wednesday
> 2 June. I've had a health issue this fortnight that's cost me most of my
> working time. I'm about half done, and the new date would let me finish it
> properly.
>
> Thanks,
> Alex Chen (u7654321)

The body is 43 words. It states the date, a one-line reason, and where the work
stands. It doesn't apologise three times, and it doesn't disclose more than the
request needs. "A health issue" is enough for an extension. Week 10 covers what
it means to disclose a *need* without a *label*, and this is a small version of
it.

## Specimen: office hours

The office-hours visit has an undocumented opening, and it's easy to write
down. Say who you are, what the question is about, and **how long it will
take**:

> "Hi, I'm Alex, from NT 101. I've got one question about the Protocol
> Specification. It should take about five minutes."

Saying how long it'll take is the easiest move to leave out. It tells the tutor
how to budget their attention, and it gives you a natural point to leave: at
five minutes, "that's everything, thanks".

## Group work

A group's first meeting sets its protocol, whether or not anyone writes it
down. Write it down. The agreement fits on one screen:

- **Roles.** Who does which part, and who pulls the sections together.
- **Channel.** Where the group talks, and where decisions get recorded.
- **Reply time.** "Within a day on weekdays" is a norm the group can check
  itself against. "ASAP" isn't.
- **Decisions.** Majority, consensus, or the person who owns that part.
- **Going quiet.** What happens when someone disappears for three days. The
  week 7 message ("I'm going quiet for a few days…") is what the group
  agrees to send, and what the others agree to accept.

The last line is the one that saves groups. Without it, silence gets read
through whatever each member already fears.
