"We should get coffee sometime."

Is that a request to schedule coffee? If you've replied "sure, Thursday at
10?" and watched the other person's face do something complicated, you've
encountered the problem. The same line can be an invitation or a way to say
you'd like to stay on friendly terms.

## The specification the protocol claims to follow

The closest thing the protocol has to published documentation was written by
the philosopher Paul Grice. He proposed a Cooperative Principle: make your
contribution "such as is required, at the stage at which it occurs, by the
accepted purpose or direction of the talk exchange". Under it sit four
categories of maxim (Grice, 1975):

| Category | The maxims, in Grice's words |
| --- | --- |
| Quantity | "Make your contribution as informative as is required (for the current purposes of the exchange)." "Do not make your contribution more informative than is required." |
| Quality | "Do not say what you believe to be false." "Do not say that for which you lack adequate evidence." |
| Relation | "Be relevant." |
| Manner | "Be perspicuous": avoid obscurity, avoid ambiguity, be brief, be orderly. |

Read that as a spec, and the majority's protocol fails it constantly. "We
should get coffee sometime" gives no time, no place and no commitment. It is
less informative than it would need to be if it were a plan.

Grice saw this coming. In the same passage, he notes that there are other
maxims, "such as 'Be polite'", that also generate meaning beyond what is
said. And he says that stating his maxims as though talk existed to exchange
information is "too narrow".

<MarginNote label="Known issue">
  The author of the specification filed the bug report in the same paragraph.
</MarginNote>

So the protocol has a published spec, and a known exception the author flagged
himself: politeness is allowed to override the other four. Nobody told you
which one wins.

## Payload and envelope

Two terms for the rest of the semester.

- The **payload** is what the speaker needs you to understand or do.
- The **envelope** is how it's wrapped: the words chosen instead of the
  payload, and the maxim those words bend to get there.

Grice ties his maxims to what he calls *conversational implicature*: meaning
that gets across without being said. The course's working version is this.
When a sentence visibly bends a maxim, assume the speaker is still being
cooperative, and ask why a cooperative person would bend that one. The answer
is usually the payload.

## Why anyone sends an envelope

If the payload is the point, why not send it bare?

Pinker, Nowak and Lee give three reasons (Pinker et al., 2008). First, an
indirect request offers **plausible deniability**: a cooperative listener can
accept it, and an unwilling one can decline without either of you having to
acknowledge a refusal. Second, talk does two jobs at once. It carries
information, and it negotiates what kind of relationship two people have. A
bare request can renegotiate the relationship when all you wanted was the
salt. Third, an explicit statement becomes common knowledge between you, in a
way a hint never does, even when both of you knew exactly what was meant.

That last reason is why "we both knew" and "you said it" are different events
under this protocol.

## When does an envelope appear?

"People are indirect to be polite" is true and nearly useless. It doesn't tell
you when to expect one.

Lee and Pinker built a model that does. Their **strategic speaker** goes
indirect when they are
<span data-quoted>uncertain of whether the hearer is cooperative or
antagonistic</span> (Lee & Pinker, 2010). Their worked case is deliberately
unsocial, which is why it's easy to see: **bribing a policeman who might be
corrupt or honest.** A veiled offer can be taken up by the first and ignored by
the second. The briber survives either outcome. A direct offer is only safe if
you already know which officer you're talking to.

Ordinary life has the same shape with emotional penalties instead of legal
ones, whenever what you want is quietly ruled out by the kind of relationship
you're in.

<MarginNote label="Diagnostic">
  Ask what the sender is unsure of --- or what the hint lets either of you
  leave unsaid.
</MarginNote>

Ask **what is this person not sure of?** The next section adds a second
question: **what are they keeping off the record?**

| The envelope | What the sender doesn't know |
| --- | --- |
| "We should get coffee sometime." | whether you want to be friends with them |
| "No worries if not!" | whether the favour is too big to ask |
| "Are you around this weekend at all?" | whether you're free, and whether you'd want to |
| "I don't know if this is useful, but---" | whether the group wants their idea |

In each example, the sender risks an answer they can't predict. The envelope
gives them a way to retreat.

### The part that still works when you both know

Here's the clause that makes the model more than politeness in a lab coat.
**Even when the hearer's willingness is known**, Lee and Pinker argue,
indirectness still buys something: a higher-order deniability that preempts
<span data-quoted>certainty, gossip, and common knowledge of the request</span>.

Even when both of you understand it, the request itself remains unsaid. The
sender can dispute what the hint meant.

That is why "we both knew" and "you said it" are different events under this
protocol, and it is worth knowing for the moments when someone insists that
nothing was ever asked of you. Under this protocol, that can be literally true
and completely false at the same time.

### In the room --- find what stays unsaid (8 min)

**Four minutes, in pairs.** Use the four envelopes in the table above: coffee,
"no worries", the weekend and an idea for the group. For each, suggest what
the sender might not know, or what either person might prefer to leave off
the record. Do not assume uncertainty is the only reason to hint.

**Two minutes.** Compare with the pair next to you. Where you disagree, write
down both possible payloads.

**Two minutes, the room.** One line, argued out loud.

There isn't a key at the back. If there were, the protocol would be
documented, and we would not be here.

## The decoder

Worked examples, each checked against the principle above. The entries are the
course's, not a research table; test them in the lab and in your journal.

| Utterance | Maxim bent | Payload | Expected response |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| "We should get coffee sometime." | Quantity: no time, no place | I'd like us to be on friendly terms. | "Yes, definitely." No calendar required. |
| "No worries if not!" | Quality: there may well be worries | I'd like this, and I'm giving you a painless exit. | Do it, or decline with thanks, quickly. |
| "Is it cold in here?" | Relation: a question about temperature, asked of a person | Could the window be closed? | "Want me to shut the window?" |
| "Interesting idea." (in a meeting) | Quantity: no content | I'm not convinced. | "What would it need to work for you?" |
| "I'll let you go." | Relation: framed as a favour to you | I'd like to end this call. | "Okay, talk soon." |
| "Just circling back on this." (email) | Quantity: no new information | This is late. | A date, or the thing. |

## Decoding from both ends

**If you decode by reading the words**, the table is a lookup until the
pattern becomes automatic, and it may never need to. When a sentence might be
an envelope and the stakes matter, ask for the payload. Asking isn't an
accusation if you take the blame for the ambiguity:

> "Do you mean coffee as a plan, or as a nice thing to say? Either's fine. I
> just want to know whether to put it in my calendar."

**If you decode without noticing**, the table is you, slowed down. Your
exercise this week runs the other way: send one payload bare, to someone who
would rather have it that way, and notice what it costs you to do it.

Sending a payload bare is always valid. Under Pinker et al.'s account, it also
changes the relationship a little, because it creates common knowledge where
a hint wouldn't. That is a cost, not a mistake. Sometimes it's worth paying.
You can lower it by saying you're choosing directness: "I'm going to be
literal, because I'd rather not get this wrong."

### In the room --- three readings, three replies (10 min)

These cases are fictional. The table is a prompt bank, not a decoder key.

| Case | Exchange and available context |
| --- | --- |
| A | Beside an open window: "I'm getting cold." / "Want me to shut it?" / "Please." |
| B | On a call comparing two rooms: "Is it cold in yours?" / "The thermometer says sixteen." |
| C | In a planning chat: "Tuesday could work!" No further message yet. |

**Two minutes, alone:** mark what is said and what you infer. Which later
turn, if any, checks the inference? **Three minutes, in pairs:** write one
reply to C that makes room for either an available Tuesday or a hesitant one.
**Three minutes:** swap the reply with another pair; they name the options it
leaves open. **Two minutes, the room:** compare two replies and name what is
still unknown.

A's follow-up confirms a request to close the window. B has a stated reason
for asking about temperature. C remains unresolved: "Tuesday is bad" is one
possible reading, not a fact in the log. "Is Tuesday a yes, or should we find
another day? Either works for me" checks it without first declaring what the
sender meant.

## Who is actually wrong when a hint misses

Keysar and Henly had people say **deliberately ambiguous sentences** while
trying to convey one specific intention, then asked them to estimate whether
the addressee had got it. They **overestimated, consistently**: they expected to
be understood more often than they were (Keysar & Henly, 2002).

**Overhearers** who had been told the speaker's intention did *not* make the
same error. So the overestimation isn't
a general fact about watching a conversation. It comes from having been the one
who spoke.

The authors' explanation: a speaker monitoring their own utterance
<span data-quoted>does not act as an unbiased observer</span>. They
underestimate how ambiguous their sentence was, and overestimate how much their
own extra cues made the intention clear. The abstract calls this a systematic
source of miscommunication in ordinary conversation.

<MarginNote label="Sender-side fault">
  The sender's confidence that it was obvious is the thing the experiment
  measured. It did not hold up.
</MarginNote>

Ask the listener what they understood. The sender's confidence cannot tell
you that.

If you send envelopes: when it matters, check what the listener understood.
Send a second one that is plainer if needed, and don't treat having to as a
failure on their part.

If you miss them: the sender may have overestimated how clear they were. You
can question the hint and still want the decoder. Wanting to get better at
something is not an admission that the whole problem was yours.

### But is the decoder missing?

A fair question to ask at this point, if you've been told all your life that you
don't get hints: is this the part that's absent?

Chevallier, Wilson, Happé and Noveck tested one well-studied inference. From
"John or Mary will come", most listeners infer that not both will --- although
the words permit both. They **predicted**, on the basis of the existing
literature and its <span data-quoted>reported pragmatic deficits</span>, that
autistic participants would make fewer of these inferences than matched
controls.

They report the result as <span data-quoted>contrary to expectations</span>:
performance did not differ between the groups (Chevallier et al., 2010).

One experiment tested one inference type in two matched groups. It does not
settle wider questions about pragmatics.

### In the room --- send it twice (7 min)

**Three minutes, alone.** Write one request as an envelope. Use a real one
you are willing to share, or the fictional request "Please close the window"
from Thursday's bank. Include the setting.

**Two minutes.** Underneath, write the same request bare. No softening, no
"no worries if not", no exit ramp.

**Two minutes, in pairs.** Swap and read only the *envelope*. Your partner
writes what they think the payload is. Then you show them the bare version.

If the bare version feels unsendable, write down why. Return to that cost in
week 6.

## Over-decoding

The failure the table doesn't show is hearing an envelope where there's
nothing inside. "Is it cold in here?" is sometimes just a question about the
temperature. Treating every sentence as a hint is as nonconforming as
treating none as one, and more tiring.

The course's rule of thumb: decode when a maxim is *visibly* bent, and the
listener is plainly expected to do something about it. Otherwise, take the
words at face value, and let the speaker send a second, clearer envelope if
they need to.

### In the room --- the over-decoding audit (6 min)

**Three minutes, alone.** Write down two times this week you suspected a
sentence was an envelope. For each, answer: was a maxim *visibly* bent, and
were you *plainly* expected to do something?

You can use the fictional temperature and Tuesday cases above instead. Label
them as practice; they are not observations for your journal.

**Three minutes, in pairs.** Read one out. Your partner's only job is to say
whether the face-value reading also works.

If the face-value reading works and nothing was expected of you, you may have
over-decoded. Note how much time you spent on it, and whether the other person
had any reason to notice.

clear they were.
