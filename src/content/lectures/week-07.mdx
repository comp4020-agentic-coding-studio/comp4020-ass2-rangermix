Week 6 kept the books. This week is about what happens when they don't
balance for long enough. Before the two-week break, you'll write a runbook:
decisions made in advance, by the version of you who can make them, for the
version of you who can't.

## Three time scales

The course separates three things that all get called "being overwhelmed". The
distinctions are the course's; only the third has a research definition behind
it, and that comes next.

| State | Time scale | What it looks like from inside |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Overload** | this afternoon | input arriving faster than it can be processed: noise, people, decisions |
| **Shutdown** | this evening | output stops: words are hard to find, messages go unanswered, the room needs to be quiet |
| **Burnout** | this semester | the budget has been overspent for weeks, and rest on top of the same load isn't refilling it |

These are planning time scales, not recovery deadlines. The table separates
an immediate demand for quiet from a load that has been accumulating for weeks.

### Where this table is wrong

The table above has three rows. The literature names **four** phenomena, and
calls them **BIMS**: **b**urnout, **i**nertia, **m**eltdown and **s**hutdown
(Phung et al., 2021). Two of those --- inertia and meltdown --- are not in the
course's table at all.

The three scales are an **incomplete map**. Phung and colleagues interviewed
**eight autistic children and youth** and found BIMS described as a
multi-faceted experience with **emotional, cognitive and physical** components,
and one **often misunderstood by the people around them**.

Eight young people is a small study and it is not about adults, so nothing here
is a claim about how common any of this is. Use the emotional, cognitive and
physical components as prompts for your runbook, and leave room for a state
this course didn't name.

## Burnout, defined by the people who have it

Autistic adults have long discussed "autistic burnout" among themselves, while
the academic and clinical literature barely mentioned it. Two studies have since defined it
with the people who experience it.

Raymaker and colleagues used a community-based participatory approach. Their
participants described autistic burnout as **chronic exhaustion, loss of
skills, and reduced tolerance to stimulus**. It came from **cumulative load**
and **barriers to getting relief** from it. Participants linked recovery to
**acceptance and social support**, **time off or reduced expectations**, and
**doing things in an autistic way, including unmasking**. The authors note the
danger of teaching autistic people to mask (Raymaker et al., 2020).

Higgins and colleagues reached an agreed definition with 23 autistic adults
over three survey rounds: **exhaustion, withdrawal, difficulties with executive
function, generally reduced functioning, and more pronounced autistic traits**
(Higgins et al., 2021).

Both studies found it distinct from depression and from ordinary work burnout.
Both name masking and an unaccommodating environment among the causes. That is
the thread from week 6: the ledger's costs accumulate, and the recovery
research says the way out is removing load, not adding rest on top of it.

<MarginNote label="Incident review">
  The fix recommended by the people who have it: remove load. Not "try harder
  at resting".
</MarginNote>

## Overload is measurable, not just describable

"Overload" has been the course's word for something students describe. It has
also been measured.

Strömberg and colleagues surveyed **98 Swedish adults --- 62 autistic, 36
non-autistic** --- about sensory experience and communication in **health care
settings**. Autistic participants reported **greater discomfort with background
sound levels** than non-autistic participants in the same settings, and felt
**more misunderstood by providers** (Strömberg et al., 2022).

The study compares reported sensory discomfort in a specific setting. That
setting is also one this lecture is about to tell you to contact. Hold that until §6.

### In the room --- name your own early signs (6 min)

Two minutes, on your own, no discussion: write the **first three things** you
notice when load is climbing. Not what you feel --- what you *do*, or stop
doing. Phung et al.'s three components are a prompt: something emotional,
something cognitive, something physical.

Then four minutes in pairs: share one sign if you want to, or use this fictional
one: "Nico rereads a message three times and leaves the reply in drafts."
Discuss what someone else could actually notice from outside.

If the sign would be hard for someone else to notice, write down how you
could tell them when it appears.

## Recovery, for everyone in the room

In occupational health research, Sonnentag and Fritz separated four kinds of
**recovery experience** from work (Sonnentag & Fritz, 2007):

- **Detachment:** not thinking about the work.
- **Relaxation:** low activation, low effort.
- **Mastery:** something challenging that isn't the work, like a skill, a
  craft or a game you get better at.
- **Control:** choosing what you do with the time.

Their research is about employees recovering from ordinary work stress, and the
course does not claim it treats or prevents burnout. It is a useful planning
checklist: a break with all four in it is a different break from a fortnight
spent half-doing the readings.

Here is how the distinction can change a plan. These are course examples, not
four activities everyone must find restful.

| Experience | A possible choice | What to check |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Detachment | Silence course-chat notifications for a chosen hour. | Does work still enter through another channel? |
| Relaxation | Sit somewhere quiet without a task to finish. | Has "rest" acquired a target or a deadline? |
| Mastery | Spend half an hour learning a guitar chord for your own interest. | Can you stop, or has it become another obligation? |
| Control | Leave Saturday morning unallocated. | Is the time actually yours to choose, given work or care commitments? |

## The runbook

A runbook is what an operations team writes so that, at 3am, nobody has to
think. Yours has six sections. Write it this week, while writing is cheap.

| Section | What goes in it | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Early signs | What you notice first, in your own words | "I stop replying to the group chat. Loud rooms get louder." |
| First three actions | Things that need no decisions | "Headphones. Eat something. Cancel the optional thing." |
| Messages | Pre-written, saved, ready to send | See below |
| Who gets told | Names, and what each needs to know | "Housemate: need quiet. Tutor: may miss lab." |
| What gets dropped | Load to remove, in order | "1. Social plans. 2. Non-assessed reading. 3. Replying within a day." |
| Recovery | What refills, from past experience | "A day with no plans. The long walk. The game." |

Fill in **what gets dropped** before adding recovery activities. A runbook
that only adds things to a full week hasn't changed the load.

### A fictional runbook: Nico's Thursday

Nico has a lab at 14:05, an optional meal at 18:00, and a paid shift at 20:00.
The shift cannot be dropped today. Nico has written this while planning the
week; it is a practice case, not a diagnosis.

| Section | Nico's entry |
| --- | --- |
| Early signs | Rereading messages; leaving replies in drafts. |
| First three actions | Silence the social chat. Eat the snack already packed. Go to the quiet study room booked for 17:00. |
| Messages | To the meal organiser: "I'm skipping tonight. Nothing's wrong between us. No need to rearrange." |
| Who gets told | Meal organiser: absent. Housemate: needs quiet after the shift. |
| What gets dropped | The optional meal, then tomorrow's unassessed reading. |
| Recovery | The quiet half-hour that has helped Nico before; review whether it helps this time. |

### In the room --- run the runbook (12 min)

**Two minutes, in pairs.** Read Nico's case. Mark the fixed commitment and the
two things that can be dropped.

**Four minutes.** Run the first three actions and message on paper. List every
fact you would still need before following them. The test is whether someone
can act on this document, not whether the document sounds reassuring.

**Three minutes.** New fact: the booked room closes unexpectedly. A quieter
library space is available until 19:00, but Nico's phone is almost flat. Revise
the first actions and where the message is kept. Do not solve the problem by
cancelling the paid shift that the case says is fixed.

**Three minutes.** Swap with another pair. They try to follow the revised
version and identify one remaining decision. Record it as a limit or make the
instruction usable. A runbook with a known gap is better than one with an
unacknowledged operator dependency.

### Pre-written messages

Composing a message costs exactly what runs out first. So write them now and
save them where you can send them in two taps. The wording is the course's;
edit it until it sounds like you, then stop editing.

> **To a tutor.** Subject: [NT101] Lab: absent Thursday. "I won't be at
> Thursday's lab. I'll work from the lab page and email by Monday if I need
> anything. No reply needed."

> **To friends or a group chat.** "I'm going quiet for a few days. Nothing's
> wrong between us. I'll message when I'm back."

> **To someone you live with.** "Rough day. I need the room quiet tonight. It's
> not about you."

Each one tells the recipient what is changing and what response is needed.
It may save a follow-up message; it cannot guarantee that nobody will ask one.

### In the room --- draft one message (8 min)

**One minute.** Pick one template above. Use your own situation privately, or
write for Nico. Nothing needs to be sent to another person.

**Four minutes.** Draft under 40 words: what is changing, what the recipient
needs to do, and when they can expect to hear from the sender if that is known.
Save it where you can reach it; a phone, a printed card or another reliable
place is fine.

**Two minutes.** Read it as the recipient. Remove a sentence only if the
remaining message still contains what they need. "No change needed" is allowed.

**One minute.** Test opening it again. Note anything that would make it hard to
use on the day. Thursday's lab finishes the set, or uses its reduced path.

## When it's more than a runbook

Both burnout studies report that it can bring thoughts of suicide. A runbook
is not care, and this part isn't a protocol.

Strömberg et al. found autistic adults reporting more sensory discomfort and
feeling more misunderstood in
health care settings than non-autistic adults did. That was Swedish health
care, not this university, and it is not a reason to avoid asking for help ---
it is a reason to plan the ask while you can, which is what the runbook's
**messages** and **who gets told** sections are for.

If you're thinking about suicide, or you're not safe, tell someone today.
In Australia you can call **Lifeline on 13 11 14** or text **0477 13 11 14**,
any time. In an emergency, call **000**. The policies page lists the
university's own support services, and your tutor and convenor will help you
reach them. Getting help to people who ask is what those services are for.
