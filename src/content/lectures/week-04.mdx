"Want to come to the thing on Friday?"

A pause. "…Yeah, maybe."

The person who asked has already heard "no". Not because of "maybe", which
came last, but because of the pause, which came first. This week is about that
pause: how long the protocol allows, what a longer one means, and what to do
when you need more time than the protocol gives.

## A system with no referee

Nobody hands out turns in a conversation. There is no chair, no queue and no
published order, and yet everyone keeps time. Sacks, Schegloff and Jefferson
described turn-taking as **locally managed** and **party-administered**: the
people talking run it themselves, turn by turn (Sacks et al., 1974).

A distributed system with no coordinator and no documentation still has to
agree on timing. It does, remarkably precisely.

## The clock

Across ten languages, from major world languages to small indigenous
communities, conversations avoid overlapping talk and keep the silence between
turns short. Average gaps differed between languages, but every language's
average fell within 250 ms of the cross-language mean (Stivers et al., 2009).
The authors suggest that people are sensitive enough to these small tempo
differences to experience them as large differences in style.

How short is short? A typical gap between turns is **of the order of 200 ms**.
Planning and producing even a short utterance takes **over 600 ms**. The two
numbers can only both be true if listeners start preparing their reply *before*
the speaker finishes, by predicting where the turn will end (Levinson &
Torreira, 2015).

Kendrick puts the ordinary transition at a **range of about 100--300 ms**, and
warns that this estimate of central tendency "hides a wealth of complex
variation": the type of turn being answered can change the timing (Kendrick,
2015). Treat these figures as typical values, not deadlines for a reply. Week
12's errata returns to this limit.

So the protocol's clock assumes you started composing your answer while the
question was still being asked. Anyone who waits to hear the whole question
before thinking about it is, by that clock, already late.

## The undocumented timeout

Here is why a pause sounds like no. Kendrick and Torreira measured replies in
telephone calls. Most acceptances and rejections came at similar speeds. But
once a gap reached **700 ms or more**, rejections clearly outnumbered
acceptances, and even small departures from a normal gap made a plain "yes"
less likely (Kendrick & Torreira, 2015).

That pattern gives a listener a reason to hear a long gap as reluctance. It
does not tell them what a particular pause means.

The course calls this the **timeout**: the point at which a listener stops
simply waiting and starts inferring meaning from the gap. It isn't a hard
number. The research supports "about 200 ms is ordinary, and by 700 ms a no is
more likely than a yes".

## The inference is general. The calibration is local.

Kendrick and Torreira watched real calls and found that long gaps tend to come
before rejections. That is an observation. Roberts, Margutti and Takano did
the other experiment: they **set** the gap and measured what listeners
concluded from it (Roberts et al., 2011).

Telephone conversations, modelled on a real call between friends, were
manipulated so the reply came after **0 ms, 600 ms or 1200 ms**. Each version
was crossed with two kinds of turn to respond to --- a request and an
assessment. Native speakers of **American English, Italian and Japanese**,
each in their own country, rated how willing the replier seemed to comply or
to agree.

**First:** ratings of willingness **fell significantly as the silence got
longer, in every one of the three language groups.** Reading a pause as
reluctance is not an English-speaker's quirk. It appears to be what listeners
generally do.

**Second:** the groups' **means differed significantly** from each other ---
different expectations for agreement. The direction of the effect was shared.
The amount was not.

A listener brings their own timing expectations to the conversation. The
person answering may have learned a different pace.

## Who the timeout misfires on

A long gap might mean reluctance. It might also mean:

- the person is translating from another language;
- they were taught to consider a question before answering it;
- they process spoken language at their own pace and need the whole sentence;
- they're choosing between two answers that are both yes.

Those pauses can be mistaken for a no. If you're the fast one in a
conversation, **before you conclude, ask.** "No pressure either way. Is that
a yes?" costs one turn.

### In the room --- guess the gap (7 min)

Get a stopwatch on your phone. In threes: one person asks an ordinary
question, one answers after a deliberate pause, and the third times the gap
and writes down what the pause *read as* before looking at the number.

Do it four times, and change who pauses. Then compare the guesses with the
times.

Compare the order of the gaps with their measured lengths. Were you better
at ranking them than estimating milliseconds?

## A gap can be given, not only read

So far the gap is something that happens to you and gets misread. Kendrick's
study of **169 other-initiations of repair** --- the moves people make to flag
that they didn't catch something --- shows the other half.

Those moves are themselves slow. The most frequent came after gaps of about
**700 ms**, which is to say the turn whose job is flagging trouble does *not*
obey the fast-transition rule. Within them, initiations that pointed at the
specific trouble came after **shorter** gaps than vague ones, and the rare ones
that straight-up corrected an error came **without delay** (Kendrick, 2015).

Kendrick's analysis of what happens in those gaps suggests the extra time is
being **used**: to catch
up on a turn you only half-registered, to produce a facial signal --- and
**to give the other person the chance to fix it themselves.**

<MarginNote label="Read the other way">
  Some of the silence aimed at you is patience.
</MarginNote>

That is a courtesy with a cost, and it is invisible if you have been taught
that all silence is judgement. Some of the pauses in your conversations are
someone waiting to see whether you want to take the sentence back before they
have to say anything about it. Week 5 is what happens next.

## Hold tokens

If you need more time than the clock gives, fill the gap with a sound that
means *I'm still here, and I'm thinking*. The gap the listener would have read
is no longer empty.

The scripts are the course's proposals: filling the gap gives the listener
something besides silence to interpret. Try them in Thursday's lab.

| Situation | Hold token |
| --- | --- |
| A question you want to think about | "Hm, let me think about that." |
| An invitation you're keen on but need to check | "Oh, nice. Let me just check my week." |
| A hard question in a tutorial | "Good question. Give me a second." |
| Anything, when you know you'll take a while | "Mm." |

A **standing hold token** makes the request in advance. Said once, early and
without apology, "I sometimes take a second to answer. A pause from me isn't
a no" gives the other person context for your pauses. This is the course's
recommendation, not a tested finding. Week 11 returns to it as part of a shared
protocol.

## When turns collide

The protocol avoids overlap, but it happens. When two people start at once,
one of them stops, and a quick repair puts the conversation back:

- "Sorry, go ahead."
- "No, you go."
- "You first. Mine can wait."

Use whichever fits your speech, then leave the turn to the other person.
Week 5 covers repair.
