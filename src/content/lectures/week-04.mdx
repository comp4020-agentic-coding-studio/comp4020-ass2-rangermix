"Want to come to the thing on Friday?"

A pause. "…Yeah, maybe."

The person who asked has already heard "no". Not because of "maybe", which
came last, but because of the pause, which came first. This week is about that
pause: how long the protocol allows, what a longer one means, and what to do
when you need more time than the protocol gives.

## A system with no referee

Nobody hands out turns in a conversation. There is no chair, no queue and no
published order, and yet everyone keeps time. Sacks, Schegloff and Jefferson
described turn-taking as **locally managed** and **party-administered**: the
people talking run it themselves, turn by turn (Sacks et al., 1974).

A distributed system with no coordinator and no documentation still has to
agree on timing. It does, remarkably precisely.

## The clock

Across ten languages, from major world languages to small indigenous
communities, conversations avoid overlapping talk and keep the silence between
turns short. Average gaps differed between languages, but every language's
average fell within 250 ms of the cross-language mean (Stivers et al., 2009).
The authors suggest that people are sensitive enough to these small tempo
differences to experience them as large differences in style.

How short is short? A typical gap between turns is **of the order of 200 ms**.
Planning and producing even a short utterance takes **over 600 ms**. The two
numbers can only both be true if listeners start preparing their reply *before*
the speaker finishes, by predicting where the turn will end (Levinson &
Torreira, 2015).

So the protocol's clock assumes you started composing your answer while the
question was still being asked. Anyone who waits to hear the whole question
before thinking about it is, by that clock, already late.

## The undocumented timeout

Here is why a pause sounds like no. Kendrick and Torreira measured replies in
telephone calls. Most acceptances and rejections came at similar speeds. But
once a gap reached **700 ms or more**, rejections clearly outnumbered
acceptances, and even small departures from a normal gap made a plain "yes"
less likely (Kendrick & Torreira, 2015).

Listeners have learned the pattern. They aren't being unfair; they're right,
on average. Across thousands of conversations, a long gap has usually come
before a refusal, so a long gap is heard as one on its way.

The course calls this the **timeout**: the point at which a listener stops
simply waiting and starts inferring meaning from the gap. It isn't a hard
number. The research supports "about 200 ms is ordinary, and by 700 ms a no is
more likely than a yes". Past that, the inference runs whether or not anyone
decides to run it.

## Who the timeout misfires on

A statistical inference misfires on everyone it wasn't built from. A long gap
might mean reluctance. It might also mean:

- the person is translating from another language;
- they were taught to consider a question before answering it;
- they process spoken language at their own pace and need the whole sentence;
- they're choosing between two answers that are both yes.

None of those is a no, and the listener's timeout reads all of them as one.
If you're the fast one in a conversation, the course's instruction is the
week's only rule aimed at listeners: **before you conclude, ask.** "No
pressure either way. Is that a yes?" costs one turn.

## Hold tokens

If you need more time than the clock gives, the protocol has a documented way
to take it, even though nobody documented it: fill the gap with a sound that
means *I'm still here, and I'm thinking*. The gap the listener would have read
is no longer empty.

The scripts are the course's. The reason they work is Kendrick and Torreira's:
what gets read is the empty gap, and a hold token fills it.

| Situation | Hold token |
| --- | --- |
| A question you want to think about | "Hm, let me think about that." |
| An invitation you're keen on but need to check | "Oh, nice. Let me just check my week." |
| A hard question in a tutorial | "Good question. Give me a second." |
| Anything, when you know you'll take a while | "Mm." |

A **standing hold token** resets the clock for a whole relationship. Said once,
early and without apology, "I sometimes take a second to answer. A pause from
me isn't a no" tells the other person to switch their timeout off. That is the
course's recommendation, not a tested finding. Week 11 is about why it tends
to work better between people who have agreed on it.

## When turns collide

The protocol avoids overlap, but it happens. When two people start at once,
one of them stops, and a quick repair puts the conversation back:

- "Sorry, go ahead."
- "No, you go."
- "You first. Mine can wait."

Whoever says one of those first has made a small, cheap move that most
listeners register as considerate. Week 5 covers repair properly. This is the
preview.
