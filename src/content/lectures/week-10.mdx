Disclosure means telling someone something about you that they couldn't have
known otherwise. For some of you it's a diagnosis. For others it's anxiety, a
chronic illness, caring for someone at home, a learning disability, or being
the first in your family at university. This week draws on research about
several concealable identities, alongside studies of autistic disclosure.

## What the evidence says, and why it disagrees

Chaudoir and Fisher built a general model of disclosure for any identity a
person can keep hidden. Disclosure can help, and when it does, it works through
three routes: **relief** from keeping something concealed, **social support**,
and **changes in what other people know**. Whether it helps depends partly on
the discloser's goal, whether they're moving towards something or trying to
avoid something (Chaudoir & Fisher, 2010).

On autism specifically, the research splits along a line worth understanding.

In a rating study, observers formed first impressions of autistic adults. Those
impressions were **more positive when the diagnosis was accurately
given** than when it was withheld, and raters who knew more about autism rated
the autistic adults more favourably (Sasson & Morrison, 2019).

A scoping review of 37 studies found a **disconnect**. Studies of other
people's perceptions, mostly using written vignettes, found disclosure helped
social acceptance. Studies of autistic adolescents and adults found
**reluctance to disclose**, expecting stigma. The reviewers also noted that the
research field tends to *assume* disclosure should happen (Thompson-Hodgetts et
al., 2020).

And when 238 autistic adults described disclosing at work, most had disclosed
**selectively**, and **just over a third** rated the impact of telling
supervisors and coworkers positively (Romualdez et al., 2021).

A favourable first impression does not settle whether disclosure will help
someone at work. These studies ask different people, in different settings,
about different consequences.

### Anticipated stigma and the decision

Before deciding, consider both telling and keeping something private. The
following study concerns distress among people with concealable identities;
it does not price those two options against each other.

Quinn and Chaudoir asked 300 people holding **thirteen different concealable
identities** what their lives were like, then repeated it with 235 more. Four
things predicted psychological distress: how much stigma a person
**anticipated**, how **central** the identity was to their sense of themselves,
how **salient** it was day to day, and how strongly the thing is **culturally
devalued**. In the second study, anticipated stigma and cultural stigma were
related directly to self-reported health (Quinn & Chaudoir, 2009).

The predictor **anticipated stigma** is what you expect would happen if people
knew. It is not the effort of concealing, or whether you have told anyone.

<MarginNote label="Line item">
  An expected reaction belongs in the decision. It is still a prediction,
  not a bill already paid.
</MarginNote>

This suggests a question for a department: could making adjustments routine
lower anticipated stigma, including for students who never disclose? That is
a proposal to test; these studies did not test a department’s adjustment policy.

### Levels, not a switch

Corrigan and Matthews approach disclosing a psychiatric diagnosis by asking
what the gay and lesbian community had already worked out about coming out.
They weigh **social avoidance and disapproval** against **improved
psychological well-being and interpersonal relations**, and the paper ends with
a review of **levels of disclosure** for people who opt to come out (Corrigan &
Matthews, 2003).

We read the abstract, not the paper, so we will not reproduce their levels.
The five audiences and partial-disclosure scripts below are the course's own.
The paper gives us the idea of **levels rather than an on switch**; its
population is people with psychiatric diagnoses, not autistic students.

## What it does to your past

Here is the part nobody warns you about.

After you tell someone, they go back through everything. The tutorial where

## The five audiences

Telling someone is a transaction, and it is easy to treat five different
transactions as one decision. The entries below are the
course's; the workplace row draws on Romualdez et al.

| Audience | What telling can buy | What it can cost | A partial option |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Disability services | formal adjustments: exam arrangements, extensions on a plan | a record, and they may ask for documentation (that's their process, not this course's) | ask what they can do before deciding what to share |
| A tutor | flexibility in labs, fewer surprises on both sides | the tutor re-reads your participation | the need only |
| A group partner | working arrangements that fit | it can travel beyond the group | the need, framed as working style |
| A friend | understanding, and less masking around them | the re-reading above | whatever pace week 9's rate limit allows |
| An employer | adjustments; for some, relief from concealing | in one UK survey, just over a third rated telling colleagues and supervisors positively | the need, at a time you choose |

### In the room --- one audience, one sentence (7 min)

**Two minutes.** Now write what you would say if that sentence went badly. One

**One minute.** Put your hand up if the second line was harder to write than the

Nobody reads either sentence out. We collect only the show of hands.

## Partial disclosure

**You can disclose a need without the label.**

> "I process written instructions much better than verbal ones. Could you send
> the task in the chat as well?"

That sentence asks for an adjustment without naming a diagnosis. It uses
Chaudoir and Fisher's third route: changing what the other person knows. You
choose whether to share the rest. Partial disclosure is the course's
synthesis, not a tested intervention.

More needs, no labels:

- "I think better if I can look away while I talk. It's not that I'm not
  listening."
- "Loud rooms cost me a lot. Can we meet somewhere quieter?"
- "I'll answer better by email than on the spot. Can I get back to you
  tomorrow?"
- "I have a health thing that means some weeks are worse than others. I'll
  tell you early when one's coming."

Each one names a need and what would help. None of them explains why.

## The timing trap

In the workplace survey, many people disclosed on an application or after
starting a job, and rarely at interview. Many disclosed only **after problems
had already arisen** (Romualdez et al., 2021).

The trap is what happens next, and this part is the course's own observation.
A disclosure that arrives after a problem can be heard as an explanation of
the problem, and an explanation can be mistaken for an excuse.

<MarginNote label="Sequencing bug">
  Same payload. Before the error, it may be read as configuration. After, as
  an excuse.
</MarginNote>

One option is **early disclosure of needs**: at the first group meeting or in
the first week of a job. "Before we start: I work best with things in writing."
You can make it part of the working arrangements before a problem needs
explaining.

## Receiving a disclosure

If someone discloses to you:

1. **Thank them.** "Thanks for telling me."
2. **Ask about the need, not the label.** "Is there anything you'd like me to do
   differently?"
3. **Don't re-read out loud.** Whatever you now understand about the tutorial
   last month, keep it to yourself. Saying "oh, that explains the thing at the
   party" is exactly the re-reading they were afraid of, happening in front of
   them.

And one line to leave out entirely, however kindly meant:
<span data-quoted>"you don't seem like you have that"</span>. It treats the
disclosure as a claim you're assessing.

### When it goes badly

Here are three ways a disclosure can be received badly, with an answer you
can try:

| What happens | What you can say |
| --- | --- |
| They minimise it. "Everyone's a bit like that." | "Maybe. The thing I'm asking for is still the written instructions." |
| They overshoot, and start handling you carefully. | "I'd rather nothing changed except the one thing I asked for." |
| They tell someone else. | "I'd like to choose who knows. Can we agree it stops here?" |

All three answers do the same thing: return to the **need**, which is the part
you can negotiate, and out of the **label**, which is the part you can't.

These sentences cannot undo what happened. They give you something to say
without having to compose it on the spot.

2. "Quinn and Chaudoir measured how much distress would disappear if someone
   stopped concealing."
3. "A needs-only sentence guarantees the adjustment without the risks."

**Three minutes.** For each, name the source or course model it is borrowing

**Three minutes.** Rewrite each conclusion so it stays within that evidence.
Keep a useful question or option; merely appending "more research needed" will
not remove an unsupported promise.

**Two minutes.** Compare with another pair. Check that your revisions preserve
the difference between a first-impression rating, a reported workplace
experience, a predictor of distress, and an untested script.

### In the room --- receiving (6 min)

**One minute.** Read the two fictional lines and the checklist: thank, ask

**Two minutes.** Swap roles and use Dev's line. Both people practise receiving.

**One minute.** Each privately notes a step to remember. These are character
lines, not an invitation to disclose something about yourself.
