Tuesday's claim was that listeners hear a slow yes as a reluctant one. Today
the room tests it, with stopwatches, on itself.

## Before the lab

Bring a phone with a stopwatch. That's all.

## Run sheet

| Time | What happens |
| --- | --- |
| 0:00--0:08 | Pairs. Each pair gets a deck of invitation cards ("Want to grab lunch after this?") and a deck of delay cards: **straight away**, **count one**, **count three**. |
| 0:08--0:28 | **Round 1.** The asker reads an invitation. The responder draws a delay card, waits that long, and says only "yes". The asker, without seeing the card, rates how willing the yes sounded from 1 to 5. A third person (or the responder's phone, face down) records the actual delay. Twelve invitations, then swap roles. |
| 0:28--0:43 | **Round 2.** Same again, but on **count three** cards the responder fills the wait with a hold token ("hm, let me think… yes"). |
| 0:43--0:55 | Pairs add their means to the board: straight away, count one, count three, and count three with a hold token. |
| 0:55--1:00 | Discussion. Did the room reproduce the pattern? Whose ratings didn't follow it? |

## Things this test gets wrong on purpose

Everyone in the room knows the yes is coming, the invitations aren't real, and
"count three" is slower than most real hesitations. Kendrick and Torreira
measured real telephone calls. Write one reason your result might not match
theirs; that line is the lab's third spec.

## What leaves the room

A results table showing which delays changed your ratings and whether a hold
token helped. If your ratings did not change, record that too.
