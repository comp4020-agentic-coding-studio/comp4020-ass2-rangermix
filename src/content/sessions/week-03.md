Tuesday's decoder table is a hypothesis. Today the room measures how much of
a payload survives being wrapped.

## Before the lab

Nothing to prepare. Bring the decoder table from the lecture, printed or on a
screen.

## Run sheet

| Time | What happens |
| --- | --- |
| 0:00--0:08 | Groups of three, mixed by the tutor. Cards handed out. |
| 0:08--0:30 | **The relay.** The writer puts a payload on a card in plain words ("I want to leave this party", "I think your plan won't work", "please stop talking about this"). The wrapper reads it silently and says an envelope out loud, without any of the payload's words. The decoder says what they think the payload is. Score it **intact**, **partial** or **lost**, and note which maxim the envelope bent. Rotate roles every card: aim for six cards. |
| 0:30--0:42 | **Bare.** Take the card whose envelope scored best. The wrapper now says the payload bare. Everyone writes one line: what did saying it bare cost, and what did it save? |
| 0:42--0:55 | Groups report their lost envelopes to the room. The tutor collects each one's bent maxim. Is one maxim losing more payloads than the others? |
| 0:55--1:00 | Write down one envelope from this week that you'd decode differently now. |

## What to notice

In most groups, some payloads arrive intact in someone's envelope and get lost
in someone else's. That is the room finding out that the envelopes aren't
shared. People wrap differently, and decoders are tuned to their own style of
wrapping. Keep hold of that: it's week 11's argument, arriving early.

Write down the costs from the bare round honestly. For some people, saying
"I think your plan won't work" out loud costs almost nothing. For others it
costs a great deal. Neither is the correct price.

## What leaves the room

Your group's scored list, and a candidate rule for Sunday's journal: a
payload, the envelope that lost it, and your guess at why.
