enough.

## Prepared examples

These are fictional practice data, not observations to put in the Field Journal.

| Example | Exchange | What can be logged |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A | Two acquaintances keep walking. A: "How are you?" B: "Not bad, you?" A: "Good." | A neutral token, a returned request, then closure. |
| B | A stops beside B. A: "How are you?" B: "Fine." A: "No, how are you actually?" B: "Worried about Friday." A: "Want to sit down?" | A second question precedes a status report and an offer to continue. |
| C | At a closed lift door, A: "How are you?" B: "Surviving." The lift arrives and both enter. | The words are known; whether either wanted a longer conversation is not. |

If no real opening or branch occurs, say so and use the examples for the
classification practice. An empty log is a result. Inventing a busy corridor
is not an observation method.

## Rules for logging people

Write down words, not people. No names, no descriptions of anyone's
appearance, no audio recording. "Two students by the lift" is the most detail
a log needs. The course studies a protocol, not the people running it; they
didn't agree to be in this lab.

## What leaves the room

Your log and a copy of the pooled tally.
If the lecture's branch signals didn't hold up, record where they failed.

Keep the log. Next week it becomes your first field note.
