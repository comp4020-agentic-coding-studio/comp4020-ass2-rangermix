## Talk whose job is the relationship

"Phatic communion": Malinowski's 1920s term for talk that maintains the connection rather than carrying information.

Better understood as **negotiated** than as a fixed, empty type.

<span class="source">Coupland, Coupland & Robinson (1992), Language in Society 21(2)</span>

```notes
- "You aren't being asked for data. You're being asked to confirm the connection is up."
- Their data is elderly patients answering a scripted "how are you?" in medical interviews --- a setting where it might be a real question. Hold that.
```

## Then he went and looked

Applying the criteria in a speech community in Western Samoa: **four types** of greeting exchange.

They break the standard claim. These greetings are **not devoid of propositional content**, and need not be "expressive" acts.

People use them to **search for new information** and to **sanction social behaviour**.

<span class="source">Duranti (1997)</span>

```notes
- Again: four types, and the course doesn't list them, because the abstract doesn't.
- The finding that survives is the one that matters here.
```

## The request

"How are you?" · "How's it going?" · "You alright?" · "How've you been?" · "Hey, how are things?"

In passing, these are **the same message**.

None requests information. Each requests one acknowledgement that the relationship is in working order.

```notes
- Five wordings, one payload. That distinction is next week's vocabulary arriving early; don't name it yet.
```

---

## Valid replies

| Reply | Token | Returns it? | Closes? |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Good, you? | positive | yes | after one more turn |
| Not bad, you? | neutral | yes | after one more turn |
| Yeah, fine. | neutral | no | immediately |
| Can't complain. | neutral | no | immediately |
| Surviving. | a joke | no | if said lightly |

```notes
- Go down the Token column first, then the Closes column. The two columns are the whole table.
```

---

## In the register of a standards document

The reply **SHOULD** return the request when there's time for one more turn.

It **MAY** skip the return when both are clearly moving on.

A returned request **MUST** be answered with a token too.

```notes
- Deadpan the RFC 2119 keywords. Don't wink. The joke only works played straight.
- The MUST is the "yeah, good" in the diagram. It is what ends the exchange.
```

## When it's a real question

The asker:

- **stops moving**
- **asks twice** ("no, how are you actually?")
- **asks somewhere private**

These are the course's working rules, not a finding. Thursday's lab tests them.

```notes
- Say "not a finding" plainly. If the lab breaks these, that is a result and it goes on the site.
```

## Why do you owe a reply at all?

Everything so far assumes the opening **obliges** you. The course hasn't earned that yet.

Schegloff has a term nearby --- *conditional relevance* --- but we only read the abstract, so it stays a name.

```notes
- This is the honest gap, and the room should watch you close it rather than paper over it.
```

---

## The puzzle, taken directly

What is it about some turns that reliably gets a response --- and when is someone **accountable** for not giving one?

Two inherited answers:

- **Linguistic**: interrogativity does it. A question is a question.
- **Sociological**: the social action does it.

Each has merit. **Neither settles it.**

<span class="source">Stivers & Rossano (2010), Research on Language and Social Interaction 43(1)</span>

---

## What they argue instead

- Different actions mobilise a response **to different degrees**
- Speakers **design turns** with response-mobilising features
- Those features hold a recipient **more or less accountable** for replying
- Position, action and turn design each contribute

<span class="source">Stivers & Rossano (2010)</span>

```notes
- We read the abstract, so don't list their features. The existence of the gradient is what changes this week's advice.
```

## Which cuts both ways

**If replies are hard for you:** you may have been treating every opening as maximum obligation. Many are turned down low. A nod answers a nod. You can match the level you were given.

**If replies are automatic for you:** you have a dial you didn't know you were turning. When you actually want an answer, you can turn it up on purpose instead of hoping.

```notes
- Deliver these as two separate addresses to two halves of one room, and give the second one equal weight.
```

## In the room: turn the dial (8 min)

In pairs, take *"You've been quiet this week."* and say it twice.

1. Once so a reply is **hard to withhold** --- stop moving, use their name, ask again, leave the silence.
2. Once so a reply is **easy to skip** --- in passing, over your shoulder, with an exit in it: "no need to answer that."

Then swap, and name **what you changed**.

```notes
- The examples are the course's, not the paper's. Say so.
- Collect the changes on the board: position, wording, timing, body. That list is the week's real artefact, and week 4 measures it in milliseconds.
```
