# Disclosure

Week 10. Whether to tell people, when, which people, and at what cost.

```notes
- The week the course is most careful, and the week with the most practical scripts. Both on purpose.
```

---

## Who this is for

Telling someone something about you they couldn't have known otherwise.

For some of you a diagnosis. For others anxiety, a chronic illness, caring for someone at home, a learning disability, being the first in your family at university.

The mechanics work for all of them, because the evidence behind them does.

---

## Disclosure can help --- three routes

- **Relief** from keeping something concealed
- **Social support**
- **Changes in what other people know**

Whether it helps depends partly on the discloser's goal: moving towards something, or avoiding something.

<span class="source">Chaudoir & Fisher (2010), Psychological Bulletin 136(2)</span>

---

## And the research splits

**Observers rating first impressions:** impressions were more positive when the diagnosis was accurately given than withheld. Raters who knew more about autism rated more favourably.

<span class="source">Sasson & Morrison (2019), Autism 23(1)</span>

**A review of 37 studies:** a *disconnect*. Vignette studies of others' perceptions found disclosure helped acceptance. Studies of autistic adolescents and adults found reluctance to disclose, expecting stigma.

<span class="source">Thompson-Hodgetts et al. (2020), Research in Autism Spectrum Disorders 77</span>

```notes
- The reviewers also noted the field tends to assume disclosure should happen.
```

## And at work

238 autistic adults describing disclosing at work: most had disclosed **selectively**, and **just over a third** rated the impact of telling supervisors and coworkers positively.

<span class="source">Romualdez et al. (2021), Autism in Adulthood 3(2)</span>

```notes
- So observers in a lab say "tell people". The people deciding whether to tell say "it's complicated".
- Not contradictory. Different vantage points on the same transaction. This course won't pick one for them.
```

Here is the part nobody warns you about.

After you tell someone, they go back through everything. The tutorial where you didn't laugh. The email that was too short. The party you left at nine.

They re-read all of it, and they decide what it meant, and they do not tell you they are doing it.

```notes
- Stop. No protocol vocabulary from here until the snap-back, and nothing on these three slides is a joke.
- Slow down and mean it. This is the course putting the tool down, and a room can tell when it happens.
```

---

Sometimes this is a relief. A person who thought you were cold for three years understands, and something between you gets easier, and you wonder why you waited.

Sometimes you lose something. There was a version of you in their head --- competent, or funny, or just unremarkable --- and it is replaced by a version with an explanation attached.

You do not get to choose which one they keep. You do not get to take it back.

---

That risk is real and this course is not going to talk you out of it.

It is going to make sure you knew it was there.

## The five audiences

| Audience | Can buy | Can cost | Partial option |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Disability services | formal adjustments | a record; they may ask for documentation | ask what they can do first |
| A tutor | flexibility in labs | the tutor re-reads your participation | the need only |
| A group partner | arrangements that fit | it can travel beyond the group | the need, as working style |
| A friend | understanding, less masking | the re-reading | whatever week 9's rate limit allows |
| An employer | adjustments; relief from concealing | just over a third rated it positively | the need, at a time you choose |

```notes
- Five different transactions, easy to treat as one decision. The workplace row draws on Romualdez; the rest are the course's.
```

## Needs, no labels

> "I process written instructions much better than verbal ones. Could you send the task in the chat as well?"

- "I think better if I can look away while I talk. It's not that I'm not listening."
- "Loud rooms cost me a lot. Can we meet somewhere quieter?"
- "I'll answer better by email than on the spot. Can I get back to you tomorrow?"

Each names a need and what would help. **None explains why.**

```notes
- That first sentence gets the adjustment and changes exactly one piece of what they know. The rest stays theirs.
- Partial disclosure is the course's synthesis, not a tested intervention.
```

## The timing trap

In the workplace survey, many disclosed on an application or after starting --- rarely at interview. Many disclosed only **after problems had already arisen**.

<span class="source">Romualdez et al. (2021), Autism in Adulthood 3(2)</span>

The trap, and this part is the course's own observation: a disclosure that arrives after a problem gets heard as an explanation *of* the problem.

And an explanation after a problem sounds like an excuse, whether or not it is one.

<MarginNote label="Sequencing bug">Sent before the error, it's configuration. Sent after, it's an excuse.</MarginNote>

---

## So: early, low-stakes, needs only

> "Before we start: I work best with things in writing."

Costs almost nothing in week 1.

Costs a great deal to say for the first time in week 8.

---

## Receiving a disclosure

1. **Thank them.** "Thanks for telling me."
2. **Ask about the need, not the label.** "Is there anything you'd like me to do differently?"
3. **Don't re-read out loud.**

And one line to leave out entirely, however kindly meant: <span data-quoted>"you don't seem like you have that"</span>.

```notes
- Everyone in the room will be on this side of it at some point.
- Saying "oh, that explains the thing at the party" is exactly the re-reading they were afraid of, happening in front of them.
- That last line treats the disclosure as a claim you're assessing.
```
