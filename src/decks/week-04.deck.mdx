# Latency and Turn-Taking

Week 4. The gap before a reply, and what it is read as.

```notes
- Do the cold open as performance. Ask the question, then hold a full second and a half of silence before you answer yourself.
```

---

## "Want to come to the thing on Friday?"

*…Yeah, maybe.*

The asker has already heard **no**.

Not because of "maybe", which came last. Because of the pause, which came first.

```notes
- That's the whole week in one exchange. The content was a yes; the timing overrode it.
```

---

## A system with no referee

Nobody hands out turns. No chair, no queue, no published order.

Turn-taking is **locally managed** and **party-administered** --- run by the people talking, turn by turn.

<span class="source">Sacks, Schegloff & Jefferson (1974), Language 50(4)</span>

<MarginNote label="Systems note">A distributed system with no coordinator and no documentation, still agreeing on timing.</MarginNote>

<span class="source">Levinson & Torreira (2015); Kendrick & Torreira (2015)</span>

```notes
- Both numbers can only be true if listeners start composing before the speaker finishes, by predicting the end of the turn.
- So the clock assumes you began answering while the question was still being asked. Wait for the whole question and you are already late.
```

## It holds across languages

Ten languages, from major world languages to small indigenous communities: conversations avoid overlap and keep the silence short.

Averages differed --- every language's fell within **250 ms** of the cross-language mean.

<span class="source">Stivers et al. (2009), Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences 106(26)</span>

```notes
- The authors suggest people are sensitive enough to these small tempo differences to experience them as large differences in style.
```

---

## The undocumented timeout

Replies in telephone calls: acceptances and rejections mostly came at similar speeds.

Once a gap reached **700 ms or more**, rejections clearly outnumbered acceptances.

<span class="source">Kendrick & Torreira (2015), Discourse Processes 52(4)</span>

```notes
- So listeners aren't being unfair. They're right, on average. A long gap has usually come before a refusal.
- The course's term for it: the timeout. Not a hard number --- the point where a listener stops waiting and starts inferring.
```

## Who the timeout misfires on

A statistical inference misfires on everyone it wasn't built from. A long gap might mean reluctance. It might also mean:

- translating from another language
- being taught to consider a question before answering
- processing spoken language at your own pace
- choosing between two answers that are both yes

```notes
- None of those is a no, and the timeout reads all of them as one.
```

---

{/* _class: impact */}

Before you conclude, ask.

"No pressure either way. Is that a yes?"

```notes
- The week's only rule aimed at listeners. It costs one turn.
- If you're the fast one in the conversation, this is your instruction, not theirs.
```

## Hold tokens

Fill the gap with a sound that means *I'm still here, and I'm thinking*.

| Situation | Hold token |
| --- | --- |
| A question you want to think about | "Hm, let me think about that." |
| An invitation you need to check | "Oh, nice. Let me just check my week." |
| A hard question in a tutorial | "Good question. Give me a second." |
| When you know you'll take a while | "Mm." |

The scripts are the course's. The reason they work is Kendrick and Torreira's: what gets read is the *empty* gap.

---

## The standing hold token

Said once, early, without apology:

> "I sometimes take a second to answer. A pause from me isn't a no."

That resets the clock for a whole relationship.

The course's recommendation, not a tested finding. Week 11 is why it works better between people who agreed on it.

---

## When turns collide

- "Sorry, go ahead."
- "No, you go."
- "You first. Mine can wait."

Cheap, and most listeners register it as considerate. Week 5 does repair properly.
