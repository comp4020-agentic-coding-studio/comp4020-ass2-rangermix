# Latency and Turn-Taking

Week 4. The gap before a reply, and what it is read as.

```notes
- Do the cold open as performance. Ask the question, then hold a full second and a half of silence before you answer yourself.
- Two hours, break at the hour. Say when.
```

## "Want to come to the thing on Friday?"

*…Yeah, maybe.*

The asker has already heard **no**.

Not because of "maybe", which came last. Because of the pause, which came first.

```notes
- That's the whole week in one exchange. The content was a yes; the timing overrode it.
```

## Where we were

Week 1 ended on a dial: obligation to reply comes in degrees, and turn design is one of the things that sets it.

## A system with no referee

Nobody hands out turns. No chair, no queue, no published order.

Turn-taking is **locally managed** and **party-administered** --- run by the people talking, turn by turn.

<span class="source">Sacks, Schegloff & Jefferson (1974), Language 50(4)</span>

## The clock

Ten languages, from major world languages to small indigenous communities: conversations avoid overlap and keep the silence short.

Averages differed --- every language's fell within **250 ms** of the cross-language mean.

<span class="source">Stivers et al. (2009), Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences 106(26)</span>

```notes
- The authors suggest people are sensitive enough to these small tempo differences to experience them as large differences in style.
```

---

## Two numbers that shouldn't both be true

A typical gap between turns: **about 200 ms.**

Planning and producing even a short utterance: **over 600 ms.**

<span class="source">Levinson & Torreira (2015), Frontiers in Psychology 6</span>

```notes
- Let the room sit with the contradiction before you resolve it. Ask them how both can hold.
```

---

## A range, not a number

Kendrick puts the ordinary transition at **~100--300 ms** --- and warns that this "estimate of central tendency **hides a wealth of complex variation**".

Things like the type of turn being answered move the timing.

<span class="source">Kendrick (2015), Frontiers in Psychology 6, 250</span>

```notes
- Treat these figures as typical values, not deadlines for a reply. Return to this limit in week 12's errata.
```

## The undocumented timeout

Replies in telephone calls: acceptances and rejections mostly came at similar speeds.

Once a gap reached **700 ms or more**, rejections clearly outnumbered acceptances.

<span class="source">Kendrick & Torreira (2015), Discourse Processes 52(4)</span>

```notes
- Even small departures from a normal gap made a plain "yes" less likely.
- The course's term for it: the timeout. Not a hard number --- the point where a listener stops waiting and starts inferring.
```

---

## They aren't being unfair

The recorded pattern gives listeners a reason to hear a long gap as reluctance.

It does not tell them what a **particular pause** means.

```notes
- Distinguish a pattern in recorded replies from a conclusion about the person answering you.
```

## Now the other experiment

Kendrick and Torreira **watched** real calls. Roberts, Margutti and Takano **set** the gap and measured what listeners concluded.

<span class="source">Roberts, Margutti & Takano (2011), Discourse Processes 48(5)</span>

```notes
- Observation versus manipulation. Say why that distinction earns a second source: one tells you what co-occurs, the other tells you what the listener infers.
```

## The design

| Varied | Levels |
| --- | --- |
| Silence before the reply | **0 ms · 600 ms · 1200 ms** |
| Kind of turn replied to | a request · an assessment |
| Listener's language | American English · Italian · Japanese |

Telephone conversations modelled on a real call between friends. Native speakers rated, in their home countries, how **willing** the replier seemed.

---

## Result one

Ratings of willingness **fell significantly as the silence got longer** ---

in **every one** of the three language groups.

```notes
- So reading a pause as reluctance is not an English-speaker's quirk. It appears to be what listeners generally do.
```

---

## Result two

The groups' **means differed significantly**. Different expectations for agreement.

The **direction** of the effect was shared. The **amount** was not.

<MarginNote label="Configuration">Same inference everywhere. Different threshold on every install.</MarginNote>

---

{/* _class: impact */}

The inference is general. The calibration is local.

```notes
- Every listener runs a threshold they learned from the people around them, and applies it to whoever is in front of them.
- Ask what a listener could check before interpreting a particular pause.
```

## In the room: guess the gap (7 min)

Stopwatch on your phone. In threes:

1. One asks an ordinary question.
2. One answers after a deliberate pause.
3. One times the gap, and writes what the pause **read as** *before* looking at the number.

Four rounds. Change who pauses. Then compare guesses with times.

```notes
- Compare the order of the gaps with their measured lengths.
- Ask whether ranking the gaps was easier than estimating milliseconds; take the room's results as they are.
```

## Who the timeout misfires on

A long gap might mean reluctance. It might also mean:

- translating from another language
- being taught to consider a question before answering
- processing spoken language at your own pace
- choosing between two answers that are both yes

```notes
- Each is a possible reason for a pause that could be mistaken for a no.
```

---

{/* _class: impact */}

Before you conclude, ask.

"No pressure either way. Is that a yes?"

```notes
- The week's only rule aimed at listeners. It costs one turn.
```

---

## And what Roberts et al. lets you say to yourself

The three language groups differed **on average**.

A pause that sounds long to you may not sound long to someone else.

```notes
- Ask how a listener might check the meaning of a pause.
```

## Now read it the other way

Kendrick analysed **169 other-initiations of repair** --- the moves people make to flag they didn't catch something.

Those moves are themselves slow: the most frequent came after gaps of about **700 ms**. The turn whose job is flagging trouble does *not* obey the fast rule.

Specific ones came **faster** than vague ones. The rare ones that corrected an error came **without delay**.

<span class="source">Kendrick (2015), Frontiers in Psychology 6, 250</span>

```notes
- Hold the 700 ms. Same figure as the timeout, arrived at from a completely different direction.
- The "specific comes faster" result is week 5's rule with a number under it. Flag it forward.
```

---

## What the extra time is for

In those gaps, speakers appear to be **using** the time:

- catching up on a turn they only half-registered
- producing a facial signal
- **giving the other person the chance to fix it themselves**

<MarginNote label="Read the other way">Some of the silence aimed at you is patience.</MarginNote>

---

{/* _class: impact */}

Some of the pauses in your conversations are someone waiting to see whether you want to take the sentence back.

```notes
- This is invisible if you've been taught that all silence is judgement. Say it slowly.
- It is also the bridge into week 5: what happens next is repair.
```

## Hold tokens

If you need more time than the clock gives, fill the gap with a sound that means *I'm still here, and I'm thinking*.

The gap the listener would have read is no longer empty.

```notes
- These scripts are the course's proposals. Filling the gap gives the listener something besides silence to interpret; Thursday's lab tests whether it helps.
```

---

## Four of them

| Situation | Hold token |
| --- | --- |
| A question you want to think about | "Hm, let me think about that." |
| An invitation you need to check | "Oh, nice. Let me just check my week." |
| A hard question in a tutorial | "Good question. Give me a second." |
| When you know you'll take a while | "Mm." |

---

## In the room: say it out loud (8 min)

A hold token you have never said is not a hold token.

In pairs: your partner asks you four questions from the list on the board. Each time, **use a token before answering** --- and use a different one each time.

Then pick the one that sounded like you, and write it down. That is the one you own.

```notes
- Practise out loud so students can hear how the scripts sound in their own voices.
- Go round and listen. Include the shortest token, "Mm", as well as full sentences.
```

---

## The standing hold token

Said once, early, without apology:

> "I sometimes take a second to answer. A pause from me isn't a no."

That gives the other person context for your pauses.

The course's recommendation, not a tested finding. Week 11 returns to it as part of a shared protocol.

---

## When turns collide

- "Sorry, go ahead."
- "No, you go."
- "You first. Mine can wait."

Use whichever fits your speech, then leave the turn to the other person. Week 5 covers repair.
