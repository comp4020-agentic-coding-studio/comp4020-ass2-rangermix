## The format has a price

Instructors shown student emails written either casually or formally.

The difference accounted for **48% to 64% of the variance** in how they felt about the student, how credible they found them, and their attitude to the message.

It also affected whether they agreed to a simple request to meet.

<span class="source">Stephens, Houser & Cowan (2009), Communication Education 58(3)</span>

---

## Two groups, two sets of rules

Same study: instructors were bothered more than students --- especially by **unsigned emails** and **shortcuts like "RU"**.

Students put the problem down to **technology**. Instructors put it down to **training**.

```notes
- That is exactly what an undocumented expectation looks like: each group assuming its own rule is the obvious one.
- Caveat it honestly: 2009 US data, and email norms drift. Evidence that register matters, not a ruling on emoji.
```

## Why anyone would care about a subject line

An inbox is a queue with a person at the end of it.

Use the subject line to show **what needs an answer** before they open the message.

```notes
- Ask what the recipient needs to know to answer the request. Do not promise that a well-formatted email gets a prompt reply.
```

## Estimating a yes

In these studies, people underestimated how likely others were to say yes to a direct request for help --- by as much as **50%**.

They focused on the cost of saying yes and underestimated the social cost of **saying no**.

<span class="source">Flynn & Lake (2008), Journal of Personality and Social Psychology 95(1)</span>

<MarginNote label="Probability estimate">What would a yes cost them? What would a no cost them?</MarginNote>

---

## Which cuts against the instinct

If asking feels like an imposition, consider **both costs**:

what saying yes would ask of them, and what saying no might cost them.

```notes
- Do not let this become "so just ask". The next section is why the asking is not only your job.
```

## Another reason to leave it in drafts

**"They'll think less of me for asking."**

```notes
- Ask which concern stopped them last time: refusal, being judged, or something else. Do not assume one is more common.
```

## The fear runs the wrong way

People hold back from seeking advice **for fear of appearing incompetent**.

People judged someone who **asked** for advice as **more** capable than someone who didn't.

<span class="source">Brooks, Gino & Schweitzer (2015), Management Science 61(6)</span>

```notes
- The paper's own next sentence is "this fear, however, is misplaced". Read it out; it is unusually blunt for an abstract.
- Keep the claim within these experiments. It cannot settle how a particular tutor will respond.
```

---

## Three things that moved it

| What moved it | What to do about it |
| --- | --- |
| the task was **hard** rather than easy | ask about the genuinely hard part, not page 3 of the outline |
| advice sought from them **personally** | write to the person, not the room |
| the person asked was an **expert** | ask whoever actually knows |

<span class="source">Brooks et al. (2015)</span>

```notes
- This is the week's only advice about choosing who to write to, rather than how to format it.
- Scope: experiments on how capable someone looks, in work and decision-making tasks. Nobody measured a tutor. Say so.
```

## The friction is at the interface

Autistic students at UK universities reported more challenges with the social side of university life --- and also reported academic strengths.

<span class="source">Gurbuz, Hanley & Riby (2019), Journal of Autism and Developmental Disorders 49(2)</span>

```notes
- The email, office door and group chat can add demands around the academic work. Ask which conventions those settings leave unstated.
```

## The institution knows

11 disability support staff, seven UK universities, on supporting autistic students into university.

They named their own barriers. Alongside lacking resources:

> "relying on students to self-advocate their needs"

<span class="source">Davies & Bagnall (2024), Autism in Adulthood 7(2)</span>

<MarginNote label="From the other side of the desk">The service's own staff call your having to ask a barrier.</MarginNote>

```notes
- The paper's plain-language summary puts it flatly: they "relied too much on students asking for what they need".
- Connect the barrier to the recommendations on the next slides.
```

## So why teach the scripts at all?

Because self-advocacy **is** associated with educational and employment outcomes, socialisation, identity, quality of life.

The review's own words are "encouraging associations" --- a mini-review, not a demonstrated effect of teaching anyone to write an email.

<span class="source">Martino, Brantley & Scarpa (2025), Frontiers in Child and Adolescent Psychiatry 4</span>

```notes
- Keep their hedge. It is the difference between a reason and a promise.
```

## But their fix isn't you

The staff's three recommendation themes:

1. comprehensive preparation and **sustained** support
2. positive communication between **stakeholders**
3. an accessible, inclusive university **culture**

With **universal design** named as the way to take pressure off individual services.

<span class="source">Davies & Bagnall (2024)</span>

```notes
- A culture where fewer individual adjustments are needed at all. That is the opposite of "get better at asking".
- Their stated limitation: staff perspectives only. Autistic students would give a fuller picture.
```

## Wording and clauses

The wording is a template. Test the **clauses** in the lab, and check the recipient's requirements before using them elsewhere.

```notes
- Say this before the table, so nobody memorises Alex Chen's sentences.
```

---

## Seven clauses

| # | Clause |
| --- | --- |
| 1 | Subject line carries the course code and the request |
| 2 | Greet by name |
| 3 | Context in one or two sentences, no more |
| 4 | **One** request, as an answerable question |
| 5 | Say what you've already checked |
| 6 | Say when you need it by, and why |
| 7 | Full name and student number |

Clause 4 does the most work.

```notes
- Separate requests make it easier to check what a reply answered. A recipient's explicit instruction to keep related questions together is a reason for an exception.
```

---

## Clause 6, and why it works

**Say when you need it by, and why.**

Not "as soon as possible". A date, and the reason that date exists.

It gives the recipient a date to plan around, and a reason for that date.

```notes
- "Before Monday, because we're reviewing the draft" is answerable. "ASAP" is a mood.
```

## Specimen: the extension

> Hi Wren,
>
> I'd like to request an extension on the Interoperation Report to Wednesday 2 June. I've had a health issue this fortnight that's cost me most of my working time. I'm about half done, and the new date would let me finish it properly.
>
> Thanks,
> Alex Chen (u7654321)

**43 words.** A date, a one-line reason, where the work stands.

```notes
- Ask before the deadline, and ask for a date. The request is the date; the reason is supporting information.
- No apologising three times. "A health issue" is enough. Week 10 does needs without labels; this is a small version.
```

---

## Clause 5: show where you looked

**Say what you've already checked.**

"I've read the brief's requirement for a check each partner can use. Our first draft only said 'reply promptly'."

It shows the recipient where you have already looked, so they can start from there.

```notes
- Alex is asking for feedback on applying the requirement to a particular draft, not claiming that the brief omitted it.
```

---

## Specimen: office hours

> "Hi, I'm Alex, from NT 101. I've got one question about the Protocol Specification feedback. It should take about five minutes."

The easiest move to leave out is **how long it will take**.

It tells the tutor how to budget their attention --- and gives you a natural exit: at five minutes, *"that's everything, thanks"*.

---

## Why "a health issue" is enough

The extension request discloses a **need**, not a diagnosis, not a history.

43 words, and none of them owed to anybody.

Week 10 takes this apart properly. For now: the smallest true thing that gets the adjustment is the right amount.

```notes
- Keep the distinction clear: the requested adjustment and the personal history are different information.
```

A group's first meeting sets its protocol whether or not anyone writes it down.

**Write it down.** It fits on one screen.

## The five clauses of a group agreement

- **Roles** --- who does which part, and who pulls it together
- **Channel** --- where the group talks, where decisions get recorded
- **Reply time** --- "within a day on weekdays", not "ASAP"
- **Decisions** --- majority, consensus, or the part's owner
- **Going quiet** --- what happens when someone disappears for three days

```notes
- Ask whether everyone could tell when "ASAP" had been missed. Compare it with "within a day on weekdays".
```

---

## In the room: write the going-quiet clause (6 min)

**1 min:** form a fictional group of three. Its draft says "Reply ASAP; tell us if anything changes."

**2 min, alone:** each writes a sentence covering what someone sends, how long others wait before checking in, and what they do *not* conclude.

**2 min, together:** read all three and negotiate a version.

**1 min:** check what each person does after the wait. Keep unresolved alternatives if you cannot agree.

```notes
- Insist on "what they do not conclude". That is the clause doing week 4's work: an unexplained gap gets read, and this sentence pre-empts the reading.
```

---

## In the room: check the reply, then the exception (10 min)

**2 min, pairs:** Sam asks: "Could we meet Friday? Can I have until 2 June for the report? Are four clauses enough?" The reply: "I can meet Friday at 11:00."

**3 min:** mark what was answered. Draft a follow-up for what remains open. Silence about an extension is not permission.

**3 min:** the tutor adds, "Keep related report questions in this one thread." Revise your message so every request is identifiable.

**2 min:** compare with another pair. Which clause did you vary, and what justified it?

---

## Agree what silence means

**Going quiet.** What happens when someone disappears for three days.

Without an agreement, the others have to guess what the silence means.

Week 7's message is what the group agrees to **send**. This clause is what they agree to **accept**.

```notes
- Check that the clause says both what the absent person sends and what the others agree to do.
```

## In the room: cut it to one (6 min)

> Hi Jonah, could you check our reply-time clause, send the week 9 question cards, and tell me whether I can attend a different lab next week? Thanks, Alex.

**1 min:** read this fictional draft and choose a request.

**3 min, pairs:** write one answerable email around it. List the other two as unsent follow-ups.

**2 min:** swap with another pair. Could a reply clearly answer it? Why did you keep this request?

```notes
- Use the supplied draft; no inbox sharing is required.
```

## The five-minute opener, in parts

> "Hi, I'm Alex, **from NT 101**. I've got **one question** about the Protocol Specification feedback. It should take **about five minutes**."

- who you are, and which course
- how many questions
- how long

Three facts, and the tutor has an estimate for the next five minutes.

```notes
- The time estimate also gives an exit: at five minutes, "that's everything, thanks".
```
