## "We should get coffee sometime."

A request to schedule coffee?

If you've answered with a day and a time and watched the other face do something complicated, you know.

If you've never once wondered, you know too --- and you couldn't say how.

```notes
- Both halves of the room have the same information here and only one of them can state it. That asymmetry is the week.
```

---

## There is a published spec

Grice's Cooperative Principle, and four categories of maxim under it.

| Category | The maxim, in short |
| --- | --- |
| Quantity | as informative as required, no more |
| Quality | don't say what you believe false |
| Relation | be relevant |
| Manner | be perspicuous |

<span class="source">Grice (1975), Syntax and Semantics 3, 41–58</span>

```notes
- Read it as a spec and the protocol fails it constantly. "We should get coffee sometime" has no time, no place, no commitment.
```

---

## The author filed the bug himself

Alongside the maxims, Grice notes others --- "such as 'Be polite'" --- that also generate meaning beyond what is said.

And that stating the maxims as though talk existed to exchange information is "too narrow".

<MarginNote label="Known issue">The author of the specification filed the bug report in the same paragraph.</MarginNote>

```notes
- So: a published spec, plus a known exception the author flagged. Politeness is allowed to override the other four.
- Nobody told you which one wins. That's the documentation problem, exactly.
```

## Why anyone sends an envelope

- **Plausible deniability** --- a cooperative listener accepts, an unwilling one declines, and neither has to name a refusal
- Talk does **two jobs**: carries information, and negotiates the relationship
- An explicit statement becomes **common knowledge**; a hint never does

<span class="source">Pinker, Nowak & Lee (2008), Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences 105(3)</span>

```notes
- The third one is why "we both knew" and "you said it" are different events under this protocol.
```

## "To be polite" doesn't predict anything

True, and nearly useless. It doesn't tell you **when** to expect an envelope.

```notes
- This is the gap in the account we have given so far. Name it before filling it.
```

## The strategic speaker

Indirect when the speaker is **uncertain whether the hearer is cooperative or antagonistic**.

The paradigm case: **bribing a policeman** who may be corrupt or honest. A veiled bribe is taken up by one and ignored by the other.

<span class="source">Lee & Pinker (2010), Psychological Review 117(3)</span>

```notes
- Use their example, not a social one. A clean two-outcome case teaches the shape faster, and it is deliberately unsocial, which stops it becoming a lesson in manners.
- Ordinary life has the same payoff structure with emotional penalties instead of legal ones.
```

An envelope marks a place where the sender didn't know the answer.

<MarginNote label="Diagnostic">Find the uncertainty and you've found the payload.</MarginNote>

```notes
- This is the week's most portable line. Stop asking "why are they being like this" and ask what they are not sure of.
```

## What the sender doesn't know

| The envelope | What they're not sure of |
| --- | --- |
| "We should get coffee sometime." | whether you want to be friends |
| "No worries if not!" | whether the favour is too big to ask |
| "Are you around this weekend at all?" | whether you're free, and whether you'd want to |
| "I don't know if this is useful, but---" | whether the group wants their idea |

The right column is a list of things the sender is **exposed on**.

```notes
- The envelope is not decoration. It is the sender protecting themselves from an answer they cannot predict.
```

---

## Even when you both know

Indirectness still buys **higher-order deniability**: preempting <span data-quoted>certainty, gossip, and common knowledge of the request</span>.

A hint you both understood perfectly is still **not a thing either of you said**.

<span class="source">Lee & Pinker (2010)</span>

```notes
- This is why "we both knew" and "you said it" are different events.
- Worth saying plainly: under this protocol, "nothing was ever asked of you" can be literally true and completely false at once.
```

## The decoder

| Utterance | Maxim bent | Payload |
| --- | --- | --- |
| "We should get coffee sometime." | Quantity: no time, no place | I'd like us to be on friendly terms. |
| "No worries if not!" | Quality: there may well be worries | I'd like this, and here's a painless exit. |
| "Is it cold in here?" | Relation: temperature, asked of a person | Could the window be closed? |
| "Interesting idea." | Quantity: no content | I'm not convinced. |
| "Just circling back on this." | Quantity: no new information | This is late. |

These are the course's entries, not a research table. Test them in the lab.

```notes
- Take one column at a time down the table, not one row. The maxim column is the transferable part.
```

## Decoding from both ends

**If you decode by reading the words**: it's a lookup until it isn't. When the stakes matter, ask for the payload --- and take the blame for the ambiguity.

> "Do you mean coffee as a plan, or as a nice thing to say? Either's fine. I just want to know whether to put it in my calendar."

**If you decode without noticing**: your exercise runs the other way. Send one payload bare, and notice what it costs you.

```notes
- Say the script out loud, at speed, so they hear that it's sayable. It is four seconds long.
- Speak to both halves in turn, and don't rank them.
```

---

## Sending it bare is always valid

It also changes the relationship a little, because it creates common knowledge where a hint wouldn't.

That's a cost, not a mistake. Sometimes it's worth paying.

You can lower it by naming the choice: *"I'm going to be literal, because I'd rather not get this wrong."*

## Who's actually wrong when a hint misses?

People said **deliberately ambiguous sentences**, trying to convey one specific intention, then estimated whether the addressee understood.

They **overestimated, consistently.**

<span class="source">Keysar & Henly (2002), Psychological Science 13(3)</span>

```notes
- Everything up to here could be taught as instructions for the person who missed it. This is the slide that stops that.
```

---

## And the control

**Overhearers** who knew the speaker's intention did **not** overestimate.

So it isn't a general fact about watching a conversation. It comes from **having been the one who spoke**.

```notes
- The authors: a speaker monitoring their own utterance is not an unbiased observer. They underestimate their own ambiguity and overestimate their own disambiguating cues.
- This is week 2's curse of expertise arriving at the level of one sentence.
```

---

## Both sides of the room

**If you send envelopes:** your estimate of how clear that was is biased in a measured direction, and knowing it doesn't make you a good judge. Send the second one plainer.

**If you miss them:** a missed hint is a two-sided event with a documented sender-side error in it.

```notes
- And the second half, said carefully: wanting the decoder anyway is not an admission that the whole problem was yours.
```

---

## But is the decoder missing?

From "John or Mary will come", most listeners infer **not both** --- though the words permit both.

The authors **predicted** autistic participants would make fewer of these inferences.

Result, in their words: <span data-quoted>contrary to expectations</span>. **No group difference.**

<span class="source">Chevallier, Wilson, Happé & Noveck (2010), JADD 40(9)</span>

```notes
- Hold it at its real width. One inference type, one experiment, two matched groups. Not a verdict on pragmatics.
- But notice which way the surprise ran, and that they printed it. That is a functioning specification process, and it is why the result is worth more than the expectation before it.
```

## Over-decoding

Sometimes "is it cold in here?" is a question about the temperature.

Treating every sentence as a hint is as nonconforming as treating none as one, and more tiring.

**The rule of thumb:** decode when a maxim is *visibly* bent and you're plainly expected to do something. Otherwise take the words at face value --- and let them send a second, clearer envelope.

```notes
- Under this protocol, they will. That's the reassuring half: the sender retries.
```

## Which maxim, and where to look

| Maxim bent | Where the payload usually is |
| --- | --- |
| **Quantity** (too little) | something they don't want on the record |
| **Quantity** (too much) | the detail they're justifying |
| **Quality** (obviously untrue) | the opposite, or a feeling |
| **Relation** (off topic) | a request about the situation you're both in |
| **Manner** (vague on purpose) | something with a cost attached to naming it |

Not a lookup for every sentence. A place to start when one is visibly bent.

```notes
- The decoder table two slides ago gives examples. This gives the rule underneath them, which is what they need when the sentence is not on the list.
- These rows are ours. Test them in the journal; that is what the journal is for.
```

## One decode, end to end

> A group chat. You propose Tuesday. Someone replies: **"Tuesday could work!"**

1. **Maxim bent:** Quantity --- "could" where a yes or no would do.
2. **Cooperative?** Assume yes. Why would a cooperative person be vague here?
3. **Payload:** Tuesday is bad, and they don't want to be the one who blocks it.
4. **Expected response:** offer a second option. "Tuesday or Thursday --- either fine with me."

```notes
- Walk it slowly. This is the procedure; everything else this week is worked examples of it.
- Step 2 is the one they skip. Assuming co-operation is what makes the inference possible at all -- that is Grice's whole move.
```

## Asking for the payload

You can just ask. It isn't an accusation if you take the blame for the ambiguity:

> "Do you mean coffee as a plan, or as a nice thing to say? Either's fine. I just want to know whether to put it in my calendar."

Three parts: **name both readings**, **say both are acceptable**, **give a practical reason for asking**.

```notes
- The third part is the one that does the work. Without it the question reads as a test of their sincerity.
- Say plainly: this is a script anyone can use, and the fluent half of the room uses versions of it too.
```

## The reply that renegotiates

A bare reply to an envelope is **valid**. It also creates common knowledge that the hint deliberately avoided.

That's a cost, not a mistake. Sometimes it's the right one to pay.

You can lower it by announcing it: *"I'm going to be literal, because I'd rather not get this wrong."*

```notes
- Announcing directness is the hold token from week 4, arriving early. Flag that it comes back.
```

## Over-decoding: who pays

Treating every sentence as an envelope is as nonconforming as treating none as one --- and more tiring.

The cost is **asymmetric**. An hour of your thinking; nothing of theirs. Which is exactly why nobody tells you you're doing it.

```notes
- The rule: decode when a maxim is visibly bent and you are plainly expected to act. Otherwise take the words at face value.
- If they needed more, they will send a second envelope. Under this protocol, they reliably do.
```
