# Interoperation, Not Conversion

Week 11. In week 2 the double empathy problem was a theory. This week is the evidence.

```notes
- Milton (2012): when two people of different implementations misunderstand each other, the misunderstanding is mutual.
- Today: what the measurements say, and what to do with them.
```

---

## Diffusion chains

Eight people. The first hears a story and retells it to the second, who retells it to the third, down the chain.

Some chains all autistic. Some all non-autistic. Some mixed.

<span class="source">Crompton, Ropar et al. (2020), Autism 24(7)</span>

---

## Where the loss happens

| Chain | Detail retained | Rapport |
| --- | --- | --- |
| All autistic | no significant difference from non-autistic chains | higher than mixed |
| All non-autistic | no significant difference from autistic chains | higher than mixed |
| Mixed | **declined significantly more steeply** | lower |

<MarginNote label="Root cause">Packet loss at the boundary between two implementations. Neither endpoint is faulty.</MarginNote>

```notes
- Read it as an engineer would: information survived within either implementation and degraded fastest across them.
- And the decisive bit: if autistic people simply lacked the ability to share information, the all-autistic chains would have done worst. They didn't.
```

---

## Misreading runs both ways

People filmed reacting to events; non-autistic observers guessed which event.

Observers were **less accurate for autistic people** --- yet autistic people were rated **just as expressive** for most events, and their reactions were described differently.

<span class="source">Sheppard et al. (2016), Journal of Autism and Developmental Disorders 46(4)</span>

```notes
- Their reactions differed. They were not reduced. That distinction is the week in miniature.
```

---

## Same judgement, different consequence

Ask autistic observers too: autistic and non-autistic raters made **similar** trait judgements of autistic adults.

The difference was what came next --- for autistic raters, those judgements **didn't reduce their interest in interacting**.

<span class="source">DeBrabander et al. (2019), Autism in Adulthood 1(4)</span>

```notes
- So both implementations notice the difference. What varies is whether the difference counts against someone.
```

---

## Rapport signals are implementation-specific

Back to the diffusion-chain videos, coding **mutual gaze** and **backchannelling** --- the "mm-hm", the nod.

In mixed pairs, less of both went with lower rapport.

In autistic pairs, **less backchannelling went with higher rapport**.

<span class="source">Rifai et al. (2022), Autism in Adulthood 4(1)</span>

```notes
- So the visible signs that someone is engaged are part of an implementation, not a universal readout.
- A listener who never says "mm-hm" may be the most engaged person in the room. One who says it constantly may be running a week 6 subroutine.
```

---

## Who pays for the mismatch

12 autistic adults on time with neurotypical friends and family: being in a minority, and **conforming to what the other people were used to** in order to spend time with them.

With autistic people: belonging, and being able to be themselves.

<span class="source">Crompton, Hallett et al. (2020), Autism 24(6)</span>

Over years, the majority's misreadings push towards isolation --- which damages mental health, and costs everyone else what those people would have contributed.

<span class="source">Mitchell, Sheppard & Cassidy (2021), British Journal of Developmental Psychology 39(1)</span>

---

{/* _class: impact */}

The loss happens between two implementations.

So the fix is bilateral.

---

## Conversion against interoperation

| Question | Conversion | Interoperation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Who changes | one side, entirely | both sides, partly |
| Scope | everywhere, indefinitely | this exchange, this relationship |
| When it breaks | silently, blamed on oneself | out loud, with an agreed signal |
| What it costs | the week 6 ledger, for one person | a few clauses each |

```notes
- Conversion is the default, and the pressure to convert falls on the minority --- week 6's pressure column.
- It goes the other way too: telling a colleague to "just be direct" and expecting them to rebuild their implementation for you.
```

---

## The bilateral protocol

Alex backchannels constantly, hints, answers fast. Sam answers slowly, speaks directly, rarely says "mm-hm".

| # | Clause | Who concedes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Sam can answer by message within 24 hours, not on the spot | Alex: immediacy |
| 2 | Alex puts requests in writing, stated directly | Alex: the envelope |
| 3 | Silence isn't disagreement; either can say "still thinking" | both: the timeout |
| 4 | Sam says "sounds good" out loud when agreeing | Sam: one token |
| 5 | Either can say **"mismatch"**, with no explanation owed | both |

```notes
- Clause 5 makes the others safe. It turns a mismatch from something one person privately blames themselves for into a word both agreed to say.
- Every clause has a check in the report: did any question go unanswered past a day, did either of us read a silence as a no.
```

## This room is the worked example

Eleven weeks. A deliberately mixed class, building vocabulary:

*payload* · *timeout* · *repair initiator* · *rate limit* · *ledger* · *mismatch*

Not anyone's native implementation. A shared protocol, negotiated one week at a time.

```notes
- It lets people who run the majority protocol fluently and people who never got it handed to them talk about the same exchange in the same terms.
- That was the argument all along. Point at the glossary: it's the record.
```
