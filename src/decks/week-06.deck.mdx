## Masking, in this course

Running a subroutine you wouldn't run by default, so that you will be read a particular way.

The research word is *camouflaging*: people do it to **fit in and connect**, through **masking** (hiding a default) and **compensation** (performing an expected response), and it has **consequences**.

<span class="source">Hull et al. (2017), Journal of Autism and Developmental Disorders 47(8)</span>

```notes
- Notice the first stage. Masking is not irrational and not dishonest. It buys real things.
- Say it plainly: this course will never tell you to stop, and never tell you to get better at it.
```

---

## Don't take that on the course's word

277 autistic adults, surveyed in partnership with autistic adults. One of three themes: **"positive aspects of camouflaging"**.

- greater **access to social spaces**
- **protection from harm**

Camouflaging "seen as necessary to survive in a world designed for the neurotypical majority".

<span class="source">Bradley, Shaw, Baron-Cohen & Cassidy (2021), Autism in Adulthood 3(4)</span>

```notes
- That last line is theirs, not the course's, and it is blunter than anything this course would presume to write.
- This is why there is no version of this lecture that tells them to stop.
```

## It's a measured thing, not a mood

Before 2019 there was **no self-report measure of camouflaging at all**.

The CAT-Q was built from autistic adults' own accounts --- not a clinician's model --- and given to **354 autistic and 478 non-autistic adults**.

Three factors, 25 items. α = 0.94, test–retest r = 0.77.

<span class="source">Hull et al. (2019), Journal of Autism and Developmental Disorders 49(3)</span>

```notes
- We read the abstract, so don't name the three factors. Say that out loud again --- they should be sick of hearing it by week 6, which is the point.
```

## The three stages, properly

Hull's model, from 92 autistic adults:

| Stage | What it is |
| --- | --- |
| **Motivation** | to fit in, and to connect with others |
| **Doing it** | *masking* --- hiding a default response |
| | *compensation* --- performing an expected one |
| **Consequences** | exhaustion; threats to how people see themselves |

<span class="source">Hull et al. (2017), Journal of Autism and Developmental Disorders 47(8)</span>

```notes
- The masking/compensation split matters: hiding and performing are different work and cost differently. Most people in the room do more of one than the other.
- Ask which they do more of. Don't collect answers; just let them notice.
```

## It works, and it has a price

262 autistic people surveyed. Both camouflaging heavily **across** contexts, and **switching** between camouflaging and not, were related to poorer mental health.

<span class="source">Cage & Troxell-Whitman (2019), Journal of Autism and Developmental Disorders 49(5)</span>

```notes
- Be precise: associations, not measured prices. Nobody has priced an hour of eye contact.
- The ledger uses a planning unit because a budget needs one, not because research set the rate.
```

---

## But the unit isn't arbitrary

Bradley et al. asked about the **frequency and length of time** spent camouflaging. Their conclusion names the axis:

> "Time spent camouflaging is what seems to be most damaging"

The "dangers" theme ties the *amount of time* to exhaustion, isolation, poor mental and physical health, loss of identity, others' unreal expectations, and delayed diagnosis.

<span class="source">Bradley et al. (2021), Autism in Adulthood 3(4)</span>

```notes
- This ledger is denominated in recovery hours. That was a guess when it was written. It is the right axis.
- Be honest that the course got lucky here, and that the guess is now a citation.
```

## "Just decide when it's worth it"

Less free than it sounds. Masking is a predictable response to stigma and to how autism has been narrated --- the <span data-quoted>deficit narrative</span>, in their analysis.

They call it "the illusion of choice".

<span class="source">Pearson & Rose (2021), Autism in Adulthood 3(1)</span>

```notes
- Their words, marked as theirs. Don't adopt the framing; name it.
- Hence the ledger's unusual column: pressure.
```

---

## And they name the cause

Asked why they needed to spend so much time camouflaging, participants pointed at **society's lack of awareness and acceptance of autism**.

Their lay summary goes further than this course ever has: reducing the pressure to camouflage "could benefit everyone in society".

<span class="source">Bradley et al. (2021)</span>

```notes
- That is the pressure column, sourced. And the last line is the mixed-room thesis in the authors' words rather than the course's.
```

## In the room: price one subroutine (8 min)

Not a whole day. **One row.**

Three minutes alone: pick one thing you did before this lecture started that you wouldn't have done alone in your room. Write all four columns for it.

Five minutes in pairs: swap **only the cost figure and the pressure word**. Would you have guessed the same for your partner?

```notes
- Most pairs disagree on the cost of the same subroutine by a factor of two or more.
- That disagreement IS the week. Land the debrief on it rather than on anyone's number being right.
```

---

## So the ledger has a pressure column

Beside each subroutine: did you run it because you **chose** to, because it was **expected**, or because not running it would have cost something you couldn't afford --- **enforced**?

A budget where someone else sets the prices is still a budget.

It helps to write down who set them.

## Everyone in this room keeps a ledger

Undergraduates never diagnosed with autism also report camouflaging --- and the more they do, the more distress they report.

<span class="source">O'Loghlen & Lang (2024), Autism in Adulthood 6(1)</span>

Long before: **emotional labor**. Flight attendants paid to be "nicer than natural".

<span class="source">Hochschild (2012), University of California Press</span>

<MarginNote label="Audit note">Emotional labour: ordinary enough to be a job description.</MarginNote>

```notes
- So the whole room runs some managed performance. What differs is the price.
- Same subroutine, one whole tutorial of eye contact: almost nothing for one person, the rest of the afternoon for another. Neither price is the correct one.
```

## Reading the ledger

Two things to notice before the numbers.

**One row has no purchase.** The switching charge bought nothing --- it is what the day's shape cost, not what any subroutine bought.

**The total is a comparison, not a verdict.** Six and a quarter recovery hours for a six-hour teaching day is a number worth *knowing*, not a number to feel bad about.

```notes
- Set this up before the table or they read the total as a score.
```

| Subroutine | What it bought | Cost (recovery h) | Pressure |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Eye contact while thinking | read as engaged | 1.5 | expected |

- Recovery hours are the course's planning unit: your honest estimate of getting back to baseline. A guess that improves.

## Five spending rules

1. Budget before, not after
2. Declining is a line item
3. Buy what you need
4. Watch the switching charge
5. Don't run over budget for long

One at a time.

---

## 1. Budget before, not after

Look at tomorrow's timetable and estimate the spend.

A day you can **see coming** is cheaper than one you meet cold.

```notes
- This is the cheapest intervention in the whole course and almost nobody does it.
```

---

## 2. Declining is a line item

Not running a subroutine costs something too: being read as cold, or asked if you're alright.

Write **that** cost down beside the decline.

Then you are comparing **two prices** --- not a price against a free option.

```notes
- Labour this one. Pretending declining is free makes the comparison dishonest, and it is the mistake that makes people feel trapped.
```

---

## In the room: price the decline (7 min)

Take the row you priced earlier. Now write the **other** row: what it would have cost you *not* to run that subroutine.

Same units. Recovery hours, and a pressure word.

Then decide which one you'd actually buy, and say why out loud to your partner.

```notes
- Some people will find the decline is cheaper than the run. That is the useful outcome and it surprises them.
- Others will find it genuinely isn't, and that is not a failure --- it is a priced decision, which is the whole skill.
```

---

## 3. Buy what you need

Spend on the subroutines that buy something you **want**.

Some of your rows will turn out to have bought nothing but the absence of a question.

```notes
- Point back at the ledger's "laughing at a joke I didn't get --- meeting kept moving". Ask whether that was worth 0.25 of an hour. Let them answer differently from each other.
```

---

## 4. Watch the switching charge

If the switching line is large, the fix may be **fewer switches** --- not less masking.

Fewer contexts, more consistent settings, in whichever direction you choose.

```notes
- This follows straight from Cage & Troxell-Whitman. It is also the least obvious advice in the week.
```

---

## 5. Don't run over budget for long

That's week 7, and it is the reason week 7 sits immediately before the break.
