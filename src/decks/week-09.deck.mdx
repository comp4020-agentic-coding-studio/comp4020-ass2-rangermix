## Friendship costs hours

Adults who had recently moved, asked about a new acquaintance; first-year students followed over nine weeks.

In both: **hours spent together were associated with becoming closer**. Leisure time predicted closeness, and so did everyday talk.

<span class="source">Hall (2019), Journal of Social and Personal Relationships 36(4)</span>

```notes
- We read the abstract, not the full paper, so no hour counts from it. Say that out loud; the spec line asks them to do the same.
- The abstract gives no timetable for an individual friendship. Keep the association separate from the course's depth model.
```

## What "hours" actually buys

More time together gives you **another chance to talk**.

The depth model asks a separate question: did either person offer a more personal turn?

```notes
- Use the two questions to explore the first scenario, not to diagnose why a friendship has not formed.
```

## Disclosure is the engine

Three effects, all significant:

1. People who make more intimate disclosures tend to be **liked more**
2. People disclose more to those they **already like**
3. People come to like those they have **disclosed to**

<span class="source">Collins & Miller (1994), Psychological Bulletin 116(3)</span>

```notes
- In the third effect, the listener is the person someone has disclosed to. Listening is a move, not a pause between moves.
- Effects vary with kind and setting. "Disclose more, get liked more" is not a rule you can run in a café queue.
```

---

## Which side gains?

Pairs of **unacquainted undergraduates**. One randomly assigned to **disclose**, one to **listen**. Then they **swap** and do it again.

After the first interaction: **listeners** reported more liking than **disclosers**.

Listening also went with greater **perceived similarity**.

<span class="source">Sprecher, Treger & Wondra (2013), Journal of Social and Personal Relationships 30(4)</span>

```notes
- This asks a related question: how do the assigned listener and discloser roles affect liking?
- Ask the room to predict which role gained, hands up, before you reveal it. Then ask the ones who guessed wrong what they were assuming.
```

---

{/* _class: impact */}

Those differences disappeared after participants switched roles.

<MarginNote label="Handshake, again">In this task, swapping roles removed the difference.</MarginNote>

```notes
- After both people had listened and disclosed, the difference between assigned roles disappeared.
- This prompts attention to both sides of an exchange. It does not validate the course's five levels or its plus-or-minus-one window.
```

## The scope, said out loud

Strangers. A structured task. Two interactions.

It tells you **nothing** about how depth paces over months --- that's Hall's territory, and this course only read his abstract.

```notes
- Keep these limits attached when applying the finding to the classroom exercise.
```

## Escalation works in steps

Pairs of strangers, 45 minutes of self-disclosure tasks that **gradually escalated in intensity**, ended up closer than pairs who did small talk.

<span class="source">Aron et al. (1997), Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin 23(4)</span>

```notes
- The word to take is "gradually". The procedure moves both people up together, a step at a time.
```

---

## And two things that didn't help

Making "get close" an **explicit goal**: no significant difference.

**Matching people on attitudes**: no significant difference.

<span class="source">Aron et al. (1997)</span>

```notes
- No significant difference in these experiments is not evidence that goals or shared attitudes never matter. Keep the result within the procedure tested.
```

---

{/* _class: impact */}

It is not evidence that jumping to depth works outside a lab, where nobody agreed to the steps.

```notes
- The study tested an agreed procedure. It did not test an uninvited deep disclosure in a café queue.
```

## The depth scale

| Level | Depth | Sounds like |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 0 | logistics, weather, the course | "Busy week?" |
| 1 | low-stakes preferences | "I can't stand that café." |
| 2 | experiences, small difficulties | "The stats quiz went badly." |
| 3 | ongoing struggles, values, family | "Things are hard at home." |
| 4 | fears, health, things few people know | "I've been having panic attacks." |

The levels are the course's, not the literature's. Test them in your journal.

---

{/* _class: impact */}

For **voluntary escalation**, the model suggests their last level, plus or minus one.

Step up one at a time, and wait for them to step before you step again.

**No minimum disclosure is owed.** Hold lower, step down, change topic or pass, including outside the window.

```notes
- Return to the examples. The queue suggests a 0-to-3 jump. For the three conversations, ask whether either person offered a more personal turn; the opening did not tell us.
```

## In the room: one turn each (9 min)

**1 min:** "What took longer than you expected this week?" Use a minor real example, or fictional Alex: a late bus, a missed library booking, another place to work. Personal content can be passed; observing is allowed.

**3 min:** A answers, B listens and may ask questions. Last thirty seconds: each privately notes their own feeling towards the partner --- warmer, unchanged, cooler or unsure.

**3 min:** swap and repeat, including the note.

**2 min, privately:** compare your own two notes. Do not infer the partner's rating.

```notes
- Practice in two roles, not a replication. Individual feelings and fictional exchanges do not establish the study's group result.
- Unchanged and unsure are useful answers. No one has to share the notes.
```

## Possible signs of a limit

- Replies get shorter --- three words where there were fifteen
- The topic returns to level 0 --- "anyway, did you do the reading?"
- A laugh where a response to the content would go
- They physically start to leave --- bag, phone, door

The course's working list. If yours differs, that is a journal entry.

```notes
- Dwell on the second one. A jump back to logistics reads as a change of subject; it is a step down.
```

---

## When you've overshot

The other person may not say directly that the topic is too personal.

If you notice a possible signal, you can **initiate the repair** yourself.

```notes
- Deliberate callback to Schegloff's skew: speakers fix their own trouble far more often. This is that, applied to depth.
```

---

## The step-down

Week 5's repair, applied to depth. Name it in one turn and go down a level:

> "Sorry, that was a lot for a Tuesday. How was your weekend?"

It tells the other person you noticed --- and that they don't have to match level 3 in a queue.

---

## Did it land?

The ladder tells you **how far** a turn went. It says nothing about whether the person you said it to felt **heard**.

A record filled in **immediately after every social interaction** --- one week, then two.

Each time: what you disclosed, what they disclosed, **how responsive they seemed**, how intimate it felt.

Intimacy = both people's disclosure, with **perceived partner responsiveness** as a partial mediator.

<span class="source">Laurenceau, Feldman Barrett & Pietromonaco (1998), Journal of Personality and Social Psychology 74(5)</span>

```notes
- Name the method: an event-contingent diary. Their Field Journal can borrow immediate note-taking without recording every interaction as the study did.
- Partial mediator, not the whole story. Say the word partial.
```

## Emotion outranked facts

Study 2: disclosing **emotion** predicted intimacy better than disclosing **facts and information**.

| Facts | The same thing, with the emotion |
| --- | --- |
| "The stats quiz was 40% of the mark." | "...and I've been sick about it since Tuesday." |
| "I moved here in February." | "...and I still don't know anyone." |

Same subject, with what it means to the speaker.

That feeling may itself be private.

```notes
- The extra clause is optional. Practise adding it when you want to share the feeling, not because the facts-only version was wrong.
```

## The move nobody expects to work

Three sets of live conversations between pairs: two online get-acquainted, one field study of speed dating.

People who asked more questions --- **follow-up questions in particular** --- were **liked more**.

Instructed askers were rated higher in **responsiveness**, and responsiveness explained the effect.

<span class="source">Huang, Yeomans, Brooks, Minson & Gino (2017), Journal of Personality and Social Psychology 113(3)</span>

```notes
- Responsiveness again, from the other side of the conversation. Two papers, twenty years apart, same construct doing the work.
- The construct as the paper glosses it: listening, understanding, validation, care.
```

---

## Follow-up, not switch

> **Them:** "I've been trying to get the stats assignment finished all weekend."

**Switch:** "Have you chosen your elective yet?"

**Follow-up:** "Which part of it is taking the time?"

A follow-up picks up **what they just said**. It can let you respond **without a disclosure of your own**.

```notes
- Tie it back to the voluntary rule: this is one way to respond without stepping up a level. Holding or declining is also available.
- Which is exactly what you want when someone has gone to level 3 in a queue and you are not going there with them.
```

## In the room: the follow-up (8 min)

**Three minutes, alone.** A sentence someone said to you this week --- dull is fine. Write **three** follow-up questions to it.

Or use: **"I tried finding a quiet place to work, but the library was full."** Remove identifying details from real examples.

**Three minutes, in pairs.** Read yours out; your partner names which words each one picks up.

**Two minutes, the room.** At most two examples: which question followed the speaker's words, and which introduced a new topic?

```notes
- If the partner can't find the words, it is a switch question wearing a follow-up's clothes.
- Let the group identify which sentences were harder to follow up and why. Do not prescribe a finding about sentences without emotion.
```

## This finding has a changelog

**2025:** a published correction. An independent audit found minor errors in how some results were reported; every hypothesis test's substantive result unchanged; corrected results and audit materials posted publicly.

**2019:** Kluger and Malloy reanalyse the speed-dating study. A second date is not liking. About a third of the variance in question-asking belongs to **the pair**, not the person.

**2019:** the authors reply that the reanalysis tested the wrong variables.

```notes
- I am not adjudicating a social relations model I have not run, and neither are you.
- Take three things: the two lab studies stand; a second date is a proxy for liking, not liking -- and so is our depth scale; and the dyad point is a claim you can test in your own journal.
```

## Choosing your own rate

Not a speed you're obliged to reach. You can keep someone at level 1 for a whole semester, on purpose.

High limit, low limit --- neither is the correct setting.

Declining escalation costs something, and it belongs in your week 6 ledger: a steady level 1 may be read as distance.

> "I'm slow to talk about personal stuff, but I like these conversations."

A hold token for depth.

## In the room: follow, switch or step down? (8 min)

**2 min, pairs:** "I'm still sorting out the new flat. Anyway, what time is Thursday's lab?"

- Follow-up: "Which part of the move is taking the time?"
- Switch: "Have you chosen your elective yet?"
- Step-down: "14:05. We can leave the flat stuff there."

**3 min:** choose or rewrite. Which words support it? What does it ask of the other person? What is still unknown?

**2 min:** "Actually, I'd like to talk about the move, if you have time." Write an acceptance, time limit or decline.

**1 min:** record what changed your response, or why your own limit stayed.
