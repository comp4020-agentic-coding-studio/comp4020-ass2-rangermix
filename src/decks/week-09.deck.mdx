## Friendship costs hours

Adults who had recently moved, asked about a new acquaintance; first-year students followed over nine weeks.

In both: **hours spent together were associated with becoming closer**. Leisure time predicted closeness, and so did everyday talk.

<span class="source">Hall (2019), Journal of Social and Personal Relationships 36(4)</span>

```notes
- We read the abstract, not the full paper, so no hour counts from it. Say that out loud; the spec line asks them to do the same.
- The finding that matters needs no number: the protocol expects hours before depth.
```

---

Three great conversations are three hours, not a friendship yet.

That isn't a verdict on the conversations. It's the clock.

## Disclosure is the engine

Three effects, all significant:

1. People who make more intimate disclosures tend to be **liked more**
2. People disclose more to those they **already like**
3. People come to like those they have **disclosed to**

<span class="source">Collins & Miller (1994), Psychological Bulletin 116(3)</span>

```notes
- The third is the easiest to miss. Receiving a disclosure well builds closeness on their side. Listening is a move, not a pause between moves.
- Effects vary with kind and setting. "Disclose more, get liked more" is not a rule you can run in a café queue.
```

---

## Escalation works in steps

Pairs of strangers, 45 minutes of self-disclosure tasks that **gradually escalated in intensity**, ended up closer than pairs who did small talk.

Making "get close" an explicit goal made no significant difference. Neither did matching on attitudes.

<span class="source">Aron et al. (1997), Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin 23(4)</span>

```notes
- The word to take is "gradually". The procedure moves both people up together, a step at a time.
- Not evidence that jumping to depth works outside a lab, where nobody agreed to the steps.
```

## The depth scale

| Level | Depth | Sounds like |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 0 | logistics, weather, the course | "Busy week?" |
| 1 | low-stakes preferences | "I can't stand that café." |
| 2 | experiences, small difficulties | "The stats quiz went badly." |
| 3 | ongoing struggles, values, family | "Things are hard at home." |
| 4 | fears, health, things few people know | "I've been having panic attacks." |

The levels are the course's, not the literature's. Test them in your journal.

## You've gone past the limit

- Replies get shorter
- The topic returns to level 0 --- "anyway, did you do the reading?"
- A laugh where a response to the content would go
- They physically start to leave

The course's working list. Test it.

---

## The step-down

Week 5's repair, applied to depth. Name it in one turn and go down a level:

> "Sorry, that was a lot for a Tuesday. How was your weekend?"

It tells the other person you noticed --- and that they don't have to match level 3 in a queue.

---

## Choosing your own rate

Not a speed you're obliged to reach. You can keep someone at level 1 for a whole semester, on purpose.

High limit, low limit --- neither is the correct setting.

Declining escalation costs something, and it belongs in your week 6 ledger: a steady level 1 may be read as distance.

> "I'm slow to talk about personal stuff, but I like these conversations."

A hold token for depth.
