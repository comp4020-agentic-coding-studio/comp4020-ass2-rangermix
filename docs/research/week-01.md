# Week 1 review --- the opening handshake

**Question.** Is "how are you?" documented anywhere, and what does the

## What the sources say

The opening exchange has been studied. Schegloff set out to "ascertain rules"
for how conversations open, and published them in 1968. Coupland et al.
treat "how are you?" as phatic communion: talk whose job is the relationship,
not the information. They argue it is negotiated rather than fixed. The same
question can be taken as a greeting or as a real enquiry, and which one it is
depends on the setting. In their data, the setting is a medical interview.

Duranti gives the week its comparative footing. The greetings literature had
been claiming that greetings carry no propositional content; he sets out
criteria for what counts as a greeting at all, and then shows a community
whose greetings break that claim --- Samoan greeting exchanges can seek new
information and can sanction behaviour. So "the reply is a token, not an
answer" is a fact about one implementation, not about greetings.

Stivers and Rossano supply the mechanism the week previously only named. The
course had been asserting that the opening obliges a reply, leaning on
Schegloff's term *conditional relevance*, which it could not define because
only the abstract was read. Response mobilisation is a matter of **degree**,
and it is built into how a turn is designed --- which is why the same four
words can be a passing token in a corridor and a real enquiry in a clinic.

## Where they disagree

They don't disagree so much as frame the exchange at different grains.
Schegloff is after general sequencing rules. Coupland et al. push against
treating phatic talk as a fixed, empty type. For teaching, the tension is
useful: the exchange has rules, and the rules include a negotiation about
which rules apply.

Duranti disagrees with the *literature* rather than with these two: he names
"greetings are devoid of propositional content" as a common claim and reports
a community where it fails. Stivers and Rossano disagree with both candidate
explanations they inherit, and replace an either/or with a gradient. Neither
undermines Coupland et al.; both make the week's claim narrower and safer.

## What survives into teaching

- The documentation exists; it was never handed to anyone who needed it. The
  course's opening joke is a citation: *American Anthropologist* 70(6),
  pages 1075--1095.
- "How are you?" usually requests relationship maintenance, not a status report
  (Coupland et al.). Whether it is a real question is itself negotiated:
  context decides, and a clinic is a context where it may be one.
- Schegloff's term *conditional relevance* is introduced by name only. The
  course does not define it from Schegloff's text, which was not read.
- **A greeting is a category with criteria, not a vibe** (Duranti). Six of
  them, proposed to make the literature's claims testable --- and the course
  uses that as its model for specifying anything.
- **Greetings need not be empty** (Duranti). Samoan greeting exchanges can
  search for information and sanction behaviour. The week states the corridor
  handshake as *this* protocol's version, and says so.
- **Obligation to reply comes in degrees** (Stivers & Rossano), and turn
  design is one of the things that sets it. The course stops saying a reply is
  simply required and teaches the gradient instead.

## Stated as the course's own observation, not research

- The concrete specification of the handshake: the standard reply ("good,
  you?"), the return, the timing, and the branch nobody takes, which is
  answering honestly at length.
- The failure modes and the lab's corridor protocol.
