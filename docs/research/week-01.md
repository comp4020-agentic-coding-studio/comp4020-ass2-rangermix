# Week 1 review --- the opening handshake

**Question.** Is "how are you?" documented anywhere, and what does the
documentation say it is for?

## What the sources say

The opening exchange has been studied. Schegloff set out to "ascertain rules"
for how conversations open, and published them in 1968. Coupland et al.
treat "how are you?" as phatic communion: talk whose job is the relationship,
not the information. They argue it is negotiated rather than fixed. The same
question can be taken as a greeting or as a real enquiry, and which one it is
depends on the setting. In their data, the setting is a medical interview.

## Where they disagree

They don't disagree so much as frame the exchange at different grains.
Schegloff is after general sequencing rules. Coupland et al. push against
treating phatic talk as a fixed, empty type. For teaching, the tension is
useful: the exchange has rules, and the rules include a negotiation about
which rules apply.

## What survives into teaching

- The documentation exists; it was never handed to anyone who needed it. The
  course's opening joke is a citation: *American Anthropologist* 70(6),
  pages 1075--1095.
- "How are you?" usually requests relationship maintenance, not a status report
  (Coupland et al.). Whether it is a real question is itself negotiated:
  context decides, and a clinic is a context where it may be one.
- Schegloff's term *conditional relevance* is introduced by name only. The
  course does not define it from Schegloff's text, which was not read.

## Stated as the course's own observation, not research

- The concrete specification of the handshake: the standard reply ("good,
  you?"), the return, the timing, and the branch nobody takes, which is
  answering honestly at length.
- The failure modes and the lab's corridor protocol.
