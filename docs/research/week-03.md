## What the sources say

Grice gives the specification the protocol claims to follow: say as much as
is needed and no more, say what's true, be relevant, be clear. He also notes,
in passing, that "be polite" sits outside that list and produces implicature
of its own. The course points out that Grice wrote down the conflict that
powers this week.

Pinker et al. explain why the protocol breaks its own maxims. A hint lets a
listener decline without either side having to acknowledge a refusal. It also
manages the relationship, which a direct statement would renegotiate. Even
when both people know exactly what was meant, keeping it unsaid keeps it off
the record.

Lee and Pinker turn Pinker et al.'s reasons into a rule that predicts *when*
an envelope appears. The strategic speaker hints because they don't know
whether the hearer is co-operative or antagonistic, and a veiled request can be
taken up by one and ignored by the other. Read as a diagnostic, that is far
more useful to a student than "people are indirect to be polite": **an envelope
marks a place where the sender is uncertain of the answer.** Where the answer is
certain and safe, the protocol goes direct. And their higher-order case covers
the rest of it --- even when both people know the answer, the hint keeps the
request out of common knowledge, off the record, and out of what anyone could
repeat later.

Keysar and Henly change who the week is about. Every version of this material
risks landing as instructions for the person who missed the hint. Their
experiment puts the error on the sender: speakers saying deliberately ambiguous
sentences expected to be understood more often than they were, while overhearers
who knew the intention made no such error. The authors' explanation is the part
that teaches --- the speaker is not an unbiased observer of their own utterance,
underestimates its ambiguity, and overestimates how well their own extra cues
disambiguate it.

So a missed hint is not evidence of one party's failure, and the sender's
confidence that it was obvious is precisely the thing the experiment measured
and found wanting. This is week 2's Hinds result arriving at the sentence level,
and the course says so.

Chevallier et al. answer a question the week would otherwise leave hanging: if
the protocol runs on implicature, is the decoder missing in autistic listeners?
They tested one specific, well-studied inference and **predicted a group
difference on the basis of the existing literature**. They did not find one, and
in the abstract they say the result was contrary to their expectations. That is
one inference type in one experiment, not a general verdict on pragmatics, and
the week states it at exactly that width.

## Where they disagree

Grice treats departures from the maxims as signals that produce implicature.
Pinker et al. give a strategic reason those departures pay. The two combine
more than they conflict: Grice explains how an envelope is decoded, Pinker et
al. why anyone sends one.

## What survives into teaching

- Payload (what is meant) and envelope (how it is wrapped) as the week's
  vocabulary. The terms are the course's; the mechanism they name is Grice's
  maxims plus Pinker et al.'s deniability.
- The protocol violates the maxims on purpose, and the purpose is the
  relationship, not the information. The deadpan line is that Grice listed "be
  polite" as a separate maxim, and the protocol lets it overrule the others.
- A decoder is possible: which maxim a sentence breaks tells you where to
  look for the payload.
- **An envelope marks uncertainty** (Lee & Pinker). The strategic speaker hints
  when they don't know whether the hearer is co-operative or antagonistic; the
  veiled bribe is the paradigm case, and the week uses it because a clean
  two-outcome example teaches the shape faster than a social one.
- **A hint stays off the record even when both people know** (Lee & Pinker's
  higher-order deniability: preempting certainty, gossip and common knowledge).
  This is why "we both knew" and "you said it" are different events.
- **The sender is the one who is wrong about clarity** (Keysar & Henly). The
  week's centre of gravity moves: a missed hint is a sender who underestimated
  their own ambiguity, not a listener who failed a test.
- **The decoder is not missing** (Chevallier et al.), for the one inference
  type they tested, in an experiment whose authors expected the opposite.

## Stated as the course's own observation, not research

- The decoder table's specific entries ("we should get coffee sometime", "no
  worries if not"). They are worked examples, not findings.
- The rule that decoding is for when a maxim is *visibly* bent and a response
  is plainly expected; and the whole of the over-decoding section.
- The rule that a direct reply to an indirect request is valid but
  renegotiates the relationship. That is the course's application of Pinker
  et al.'s argument, not something they tested.
