# Week 7 review --- emergency: overload, shutdown, recovery

**Question.** What happens when the week 6 budget is overspent for too long,
and what helps recovery?

## Sources and how each was checked

| Reading | Checked against | What it supports |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Raymaker et al. (2020), "Having all of your internal resources exhausted beyond measure and being left with no clean-up crew": defining autistic burnout, *Autism in Adulthood* 2(2) | abstract (Crossref), 2026-09-15 | community-based participatory research: 19 interviews, 19 public sources; burnout characterised by chronic exhaustion, loss of skills and reduced tolerance to stimulus; caused by cumulative load and barriers to relief; recovery associated with acceptance and support, time off or reduced expectations, and doing things in an autistic way or unmasking; distinct from occupational burnout and depression; the authors flag the dangers of teaching autistic people to mask |
| Higgins et al. (2021), Defining autistic burnout through experts by lived experience: Grounded Delphi method investigating #AutisticBurnout, *Autism* 25(8) | abstract (Crossref), 2026-09-15 | a definition co-produced with 23 autistic adults: exhaustion, withdrawal, executive function problems, reduced functioning, more pronounced autistic traits; distinct from depression and non-autistic burnout; anecdotally driven by masking and an unaccommodating world |
| Sonnentag & Fritz (2007), The Recovery Experience Questionnaire, *Journal of Occupational Health Psychology* 12(3) | abstract (OpenAlex), 2026-09-15 | four distinguishable recovery experiences from work: psychological detachment, relaxation, mastery and control (N = 930 across calibration and cross-validation samples) |

## What the sources say

Autistic burnout is defined by the people who have it, and the definitions
agree: exhaustion, withdrawal, lost skills, lower tolerance. Both studies say
it is not the same thing as work burnout or depression. The cause is load that
accumulates with no relief, and masking is named as a contributor. Recovery
comes from less load, acceptance, and permission to stop masking.

For everyone in the room, the occupational recovery literature separates four
recovery experiences: detachment, relaxation, mastery and control. The course
uses them as a checklist for the break.

## Where they disagree

- Raymaker et al. and Higgins et al. agree closely. Higgins et al. add
  executive function problems and withdrawal to the characterisation.
- Sonnentag & Fritz study employed adults recovering from ordinary work
  stress. The course does not claim their four experiences treat, prevent or
  shorten autistic burnout. They offer a planning vocabulary, nothing more.

## What survives into teaching

- Overload (this afternoon), shutdown (this evening) and burnout (this
  semester) are different time scales. The course uses the terms plainly and
  cites the definition only for burnout, the one with research behind it.
- *Runbook* is the week's term: decisions made in advance, while you can make
  them, for the moment you can't.
- Pre-written messages, because composing a message costs exactly what is
  running out.
- Recovery needs load removed, not rest added on top of the same load
  (Raymaker et al.), and the break is when that happens.
- Detachment, relaxation, mastery and control as a recovery checklist that
  works for everyone (Sonnentag & Fritz).

## Stated as the course's own observation, not research

- The overload / shutdown / burnout time-scale distinction as the course
  draws it.
- The runbook format and the message templates.
- The placement before the break, which is a course-design decision.
- The week's safety line: a runbook is not care. If burnout brings thoughts
  of suicide, that is an emergency for a person, not a protocol. Raymaker et
  al. and Higgins et al. both report suicidality, which is why the page says
  so plainly and points to support.
