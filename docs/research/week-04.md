# Week 4 review --- latency and turn-taking

**Question.** How long is the gap the protocol allows, and what does a
longer one mean to the listener?

## What the sources say

The protocol's timing is tight and nearly universal. The typical gap is about
200 ms. That is less than it takes to plan a sentence, so listeners start
preparing their reply before the speaker finishes. Once a gap reaches around
700 ms, a "no" becomes clearly more likely than a "yes". So a listener who has
heard a lot of conversation will read a long pause as a refusal on its way.

Kendrick supplies two things the week was missing. First, a cited range rather
than a single figure --- ~100--300 ms --- together with the author's own
statement that the central tendency hides complex variation, which is exactly
what the course's errata entry E-1 says about its timeout. Second, and more
useful in teaching: a delay before a repair initiation is partly *deliberate*,
one of its uses being to give the other speaker room to fix the trouble
themselves. The week had been treating a gap only as something a listener
reads. It is also something a listener can give.

Roberts, Margutti and Takano add the piece the week most needed: an
experiment rather than a corpus. Kendrick and Torreira observed that long gaps
tend to precede rejections; Roberts et al. manipulated the gap and measured
what listeners concluded. Judgements of willingness fell as silence lengthened
in all three language groups, so the inference is not an English-speaker's
quirk. But the groups' means differed, so what counts as a *long* silence is
calibrated locally.

## Where they disagree

- Stivers et al. stress universality. The ethnographic tradition they test
  reported big cultural differences, and they argue those are perceived rather
  than measured.
- Kendrick & Torreira qualify the conversation-analysis claim that
  dispreferred responses are simply delayed: most cases of either kind didn't
  differ in timing. The effect shows up at the long end, and in how the turn
  is built.

## What survives into teaching

- *Latency* is the gap; *timeout* is the course's word for the point at which
  a listener starts inferring meaning from it. Research supports "about
  200 ms is ordinary" and "700 ms and over leans towards no". The timeout is
  not a hard number, and the course says so.
- Why a pause reads as disagreement: listeners have learned, correctly, that
  long gaps come before refusals. The inference is statistical, and it misfires
  on anyone whose long gap means thinking.
- Hold tokens. If you need more than a few hundred milliseconds, the protocol
  accepts an audible placeholder ("hm, let me think"). The script is the
  course's; the reason it works is that it fills the gap a listener would
  otherwise read (Kendrick & Torreira).
- **The inference is general; the calibration is local** (Roberts, Margutti &
  Takano). Every group read longer silence as less willingness, and every
  group set the dial somewhere slightly different. This is the strongest
  evidence in the week for its own argument: a listener's timeout is a
  learned local expectation being applied to a person who may not share it.
- The tested silence lengths were 0, 600 and 1200 ms. The course quotes those
  three and does not interpolate between them.
- **The ordinary gap is a range, and the range hides variation** (Kendrick).
  The week quotes ~100--300 ms with the variation caveat attached, rather than
  a single tidy number.
- **A gap can be given, not just read** (Kendrick). Among the uses of the extra
  time before a repair initiation is letting the other speaker resolve the
  trouble independently. This is the week's one piece of good news for slow
  responders, and it is the bridge into week 5.

## Stated as the course's own observation, not research

- The specific hold-token scripts, and the claim that saying up front "I take
  a second to answer" resets a listener's timeout.
- Anything about autistic processing time specifically. No verified source
  was found this week, and the course does not assert one.
