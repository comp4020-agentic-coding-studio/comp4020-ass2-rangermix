# Week 10 review --- disclosure

**Question.** What does telling someone about a diagnosis, or a need, actually
do, and what does the evidence say about whether, when and to whom?

## Sources and how each was checked

| Reading | Checked against | What it supports |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Chaudoir & Fisher (2010), The disclosure processes model, *Psychological Bulletin* 136(2) | abstract (OpenAlex), 2026-09-15 | a model for concealable stigmatised identities in general: the discloser's goals (approach or avoidance) moderate the effects of disclosure, which run through three processes: alleviation of inhibition, social support, and changes in social information |
| Sasson & Morrison (2019), First impressions of adults with autism improve with diagnostic disclosure and increased autism knowledge of peers, *Autism* 23(1) | abstract (Crossref), 2026-09-15 | observers' first impressions of autistic adults were more positive when the diagnosis was given accurately than when it wasn't; raters' knowledge of autism was associated with more favourable impressions |
| Thompson-Hodgetts et al. (2020), Helpful or harmful? A scoping review of perceptions and outcomes of autism diagnostic disclosure to others, *Research in Autism Spectrum Disorders* 77 | abstract (Semantic Scholar), 2026-09-15 | 37 studies: a disconnect. Others, mostly assessed through vignettes, see disclosure as positive; autistic adolescents and adults report reluctance, anticipating negative outcomes and stigma; the literature itself tends to assume disclosure should happen |
| Romualdez et al. (2021), "People might understand me better": diagnostic disclosure experiences of autistic individuals in the workplace, *Autism in Adulthood* 3(2) | abstract (Crossref), 2026-09-15 | 238 autistic adults: most disclosed selectively; many disclosed after starting a job or after problems arose (retrospective disclosure), rarely at interview; just over a third rated the impact of disclosing to supervisors and coworkers positively; adjustments were rated less positively still; organisations should carry more of the responsibility |

## What the sources say

Disclosure is a transaction with an audience, and the evidence depends on
which side you ask. In rating studies, observers think better of an autistic
person once they know (Sasson & Morrison). People who actually disclose report
something more mixed: selective disclosure, disclosure after things go wrong,
and a minority rating the outcome positively (Romualdez et al.). The scoping
review names the gap and the research field's own bias towards "disclose"
(Thompson-Hodgetts et al.). Chaudoir & Fisher explain why disclosure can help
at all, and why the discloser's goal changes what happens.

## Where they disagree

This is the week's central fact. Vignette and rating studies say disclosure
helps; lived-experience studies say it is costly and uncertain. The course
teaches the disagreement, not a verdict.

## What survives into teaching

- *Irreversibility*: the one property all the sources imply and none states
  outright. The course states it as its own point, in the register break.
- *The five audiences* as different transactions: the design doc's list
  (disability services, a tutor, a group partner, a friend, an employer),
  informed by Romualdez et al.'s findings on who people told.
- *Partial disclosure*: disclosing a need without the label. It follows from
  Chaudoir & Fisher's point that disclosure changes social information, and
  lets the student choose which information. The course's own synthesis; no
  source tests it directly.
- *The timing trap*: retrospective disclosure, after a problem, is common
  (Romualdez et al.). The trap is that disclosure then reads as an excuse. The
  last clause is the course's.
