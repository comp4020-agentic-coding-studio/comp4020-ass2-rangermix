## C5.1 --- Kirchhoff, Wagner & Strack (2012) — DIRECT HIT ON THE GAP

**Citation.** Kirchhoff, J., Wagner, U., & Strack, M. (2012). Apologies:
Words of magic? The role of verbal components, anger reduction, and offence
severity. *Peace and Conflict: Journal of Peace Psychology*, 18(2), 109–130.

**DOI.** `10.1037/a0028092` · Crossref cites: 79 · journal-article

**Abstract source.** OpenAlex, reconstructed from `abstract_inverted_index`.

> Across various disciplines apologies are discussed as an instrument of
> conflict transformation. Questions regarding "how" and "why" apologies
> contribute to resolving conflicts need to be illuminated further. These
> questions are addressed in two experimental vignette studies in Germany.
> Study 1 supports the idea that the inclusion of more verbal elements in an
> apology increases forgiveness, especially after a more severe offense. The
> study also reveals that the relationship between the completeness of apology
> and forgiveness is mediated by anger reduction. Study 2 demonstrates that
> for a more severe offense four elements of apology are particularly
> relevant, namely conveying emotions, admitting fault, a statement of apology
> such as "I apologize," and an attempt at explanation. Implications for
> conflict transformation and further scholarly inquiries are discussed.

**What the week could claim from this abstract alone.**
1. More verbal elements in an apology increased forgiveness, **especially after
   a more severe offence** (Study 1) — independent replication of the
   more-components-is-better finding the week currently gets only from Lewicki.
2. The link between apology completeness and forgiveness was **mediated by
   anger reduction** — a mechanism, which the week currently has none of.
3. For a more severe offence, **four** elements were particularly relevant:
   conveying emotions, admitting fault, a statement of apology such as "I
   apologize," and an attempt at explanation.

**⚠️ This partly contradicts the course's current template.** Two conflicts the
parent should decide about deliberately:
- The week's template has no "conveying emotions" line and no "I apologize"
  line; its five parts are name it / own it / say what it cost them / say what
  happens now / offer a fix.
- The week currently says to keep an explanation *out* of the apology ("Two
  things the template doesn't contain: 'but', and an explanation that runs
  longer than the apology"). Kirchhoff et al. found **an attempt at
  explanation** among the four elements that mattered for severe offences.
  The course's advice is not thereby wrong — theirs is a German vignette
  study, and the course's objection is to explanations that *run longer than
  the apology* — but the tension is real and should be stated rather than
  quietly dropped. This is exactly the kind of "where they disagree" row the
  review format exists for.
- These are two vignette studies in Germany. Generalisation is a live caveat.

## C5.2 --- Fehr, Gelfand & Nag (2010)

**Citation.** Fehr, R., Gelfand, M. J., & Nag, M. (2010). The road to
forgiveness: A meta-analytic synthesis of its situational and dispositional
correlates. *Psychological Bulletin*, 136(5), 894–914.

**DOI.** `10.1037/a0019993` · Crossref cites: 588 · journal-article

**Abstract source.** OpenAlex, reconstructed from `abstract_inverted_index`.

> Forgiveness has received widespread attention among psychologists from
> social, personality, clinical, developmental, and organizational perspectives
> alike. Despite great progress, the forgiveness literature has witnessed few
> attempts at empirical integration. Toward this end, we meta-analyze results
> from 175 studies and 26,006 participants to examine the correlates of
> interpersonal forgiveness (i.e., forgiveness of a single offender by a single
> victim). A tripartite forgiveness typology is proposed, encompassing victims'
> (a) cognitions, (b) affect, and (c) constraints following offense, with each
> consisting of situational and dispositional components. We tested hypotheses
> with respect to 22 distinct constructs, as correlates of forgiveness, that
> have been measured across different fields within psychology. We also
> evaluated key sample and study characteristics, including gender, age, time,
> and methodology as main effects and moderators. Results highlight the
> multifaceted nature of forgiveness. Variables with particularly notable
> effects include intent (r = -.49), state empathy (r = .51), apology (r =
> .42), and state anger (r = -.41). Consistent with previous theory,
> situational constructs are shown to account for greater variance in
> forgiveness than victim dispositions, although within-category differences
> are considerable. Sample and study characteristics yielded negligible effects
> on forgiveness, despite previous theorizing to the contrary: The effect of
> gender was nonsignificant (r = .01), and the effect of age was negligible (r
> = .06). Preliminary evidence suggests that methodology may exhibit some
> moderating effects. Scenario methodologies led to enhanced effects for
> cognitions; recall methodologies led to enhanced effects for affect.

**What the week could claim from this abstract alone.** From 175 studies and
26,006 participants, **apology correlates with forgiveness at r = .42** —
a real number the week can put on the subroutine working at all. Also
available: state empathy r = .51, intent r = −.49, state anger r = −.41; and
**situational constructs accounted for more variance in forgiveness than the
victim's dispositions**. Gender was nonsignificant (r = .01) and age negligible
(r = .06).

**Why it matters.** The week currently asserts the apology is worth running
without any effect size. And "situational beats dispositional" is on-argument
for the whole course: what you *do* in the situation matters more than what
kind of person the other party is.

## C5.3 --- Kendrick (2015), *Frontiers in Psychology* — ALSO SERVES WEEK 4

**Citation.** Kendrick, K. H. (2015). The intersection of turn-taking and
repair: the timing of other-initiations of repair in conversation.
*Frontiers in Psychology*, 6, 250.

**DOI.** `10.3389/fpsyg.2015.00250` · Crossref cites: 51 · open access

**Abstract source.** OpenAlex, reconstructed from `abstract_inverted_index`.

> The transitions between turns at talk in conversation tend to occur quickly,
> with only a slight gap of ∼100-300 ms between them. This estimate of central
> tendency, however, hides a wealth of complex variation, as a number of
> factors, such as the type of turns involved, have been shown to influence the
> timing of turn transitions. This article considers one specific type of turn
> that does not conform to the statistical trend, namely turns that deal with
> troubles of speaking, hearing, and understanding, known as other-initiations
> of repair (OIR). The results of a quantitative analysis of 169 OIRs in
> face-to-face conversation reveal that the most frequent cases occur after
> gaps of ∼700 ms. Furthermore, OIRs that locate a source of trouble in a prior
> turn specifically tend to occur after shorter gaps than those that do not,
> and those that correct errors in a prior turn, while rare, tend to occur
> without delay. An analysis of the transitions before OIRs, using methods of
> conversation analysis, suggests that speakers use the extra time (i) to
> search for a late recognition of the problematic turn, (ii) to provide an
> opportunity for the speaker of the problematic turn to resolve the trouble
> independently, and (iii) to produce visual signals, such as facial gestures.
> In light of these results, it is argued that OIRs take priority over other
> turns at talk in conversation and therefore are not subject to the same rules
> and constraints that motivate fast turn transitions in general.

**What the week could claim from this abstract alone.** This is the richest
single find in the batch.
1. **169 OIRs analysed; the most frequent occur after gaps of ~700 ms** — the
   same order as week 4's timeout, from a different phenomenon. A delayed reply
   is often a repair being assembled, not a refusal.
2. **OIRs that locate the trouble source specifically occur after shorter gaps**
   than open ones — which is independent support for week 5's central
   instruction, "say the part you got." The specific initiator is not only
   cheaper for the other person, it arrives faster.
3. **Error corrections, though rare, occur without delay.**
4. Speakers use the extra time partly **to give the other speaker a chance to
   resolve the trouble themselves** — a mechanism for the week's
   "the speaker gets first go," which it currently supports only by citing
   Schegloff's preference for self-repair.
5. **OIRs take priority** and are not bound by the usual fast-transition
   constraints — so a pause before a repair is licensed, not a violation.

**Cross-week note.** Its opening sentences (∼100–300 ms; central tendency
hiding variation; turn type influencing timing) are verified evidence for
week 4's gap (b), where the candidate found for that purpose (Heldner &
Edlund) has only a truncated abstract.

## C5.4 --- Kendrick (2015), *Open Linguistics*

**Citation.** Kendrick, K. H. (2015). Other-initiated repair in English.
*Open Linguistics*, 1(1), 164–190.

**DOI.** `10.2478/opli-2014-0009` · Crossref cites: 82 · open access

**Abstract source.** Crossref (verbatim, including its leading "Abstract").

> Abstract The practices of other-initiation of repair provide speakers with a
> set of solutions to one of the most basic problems in conversation: troubles
> of speaking, hearing, and understanding. Based on a collection of 227 cases
> systematically identified in a corpus of English conversation, this article
> describes the formats and practices of other-initiations of repair attested
> in the corpus and reports their quantitative distribution. In addition to
> straight other-initiations of repair, the identification of all possible
> cases also yielded a substantial proportion in which speakers use
> other-initiations to perform other actions, including non-serious actions,
> such as jokes and teases, preliminaries to dispreferred responses, and
> displays of surprise and disbelief. A distinction is made between
> otherinitiations that perform additional actions concurrently and those that
> formally resemble straight other-initiations but analyzably do not initiate
> repair as an action.

**What the week could claim from this abstract alone.** 227 cases in an English
corpus, with formats and their quantitative distribution described. And the
teachable complication: **a substantial proportion of other-initiations are
not doing repair at all** — they are jokes, teases, preliminaries to a
dispreferred response ("you want it by *Friday*?" as a run-up to "no"), or
displays of surprise and disbelief.

**Why it matters.** This is week 5's version of week 3's over-decoding
section, and the week currently has nothing like it. "Sorry, what?" can be a
refusal warming up. A student learning to read repair initiators needs to know
the form is reused for other jobs — and the paper draws exactly that
distinction.
