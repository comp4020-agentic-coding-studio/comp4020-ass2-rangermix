## ⚠️ C1 is not a new source — it is the existing one, identified

**Read this before adding anything.** The course's existing reading
`hall-2019-hours-friendship.md` records the venue as *Journal of Social and
Personal Relationships*, 36(4), 1278–1296. The DOI below has **exactly that
volume, issue and page range** and is titled "How many hours does it take to
make a friend?".

**Citation.** Hall, J. A. (2018). How many hours does it take to make a
friend? *Journal of Social and Personal Relationships*, 36(4), 1278–1296.

**DOI.** `10.1177/0265407518761225` — 121 citations.

Crossref gives the issued year as **2018** while the issue itself is 36(4),
which the course dates 2019 — the usual online-first/issue split. **Almost
certainly the same paper the week already cites.** Do not add it as a second
reading; check the existing file's DOI and year instead.

**Abstract (verbatim, Crossref).**

> The question of this investigation is, how many hours does it take to make a
> new friend? Drawing from Dunbar's social brain hypothesis and Communicate Bond
> Belong theory, friendship status was examined as a function of hours together,
> shared activities, and everyday talk. In Study 1, MTurk participants ( N =
> 355) who had recently relocated estimated time spent with a new acquaintance.
> Hours together was associated with closer friendships. Time spent engaging in
> leisure activities also predicted closeness. In Study 2, first-year students (
> N = 112) reported the number of hours spent with two new acquaintances three
> times over 9 weeks. Hours together was associated changes in closeness between
> waves. Two types of everyday talk predicted changes in closeness.

("was associated changes" is the Crossref record's own wording.)

**The week's existing caution is correct, and now provably so.** The paper is
*titled* with the hours question and its abstract **states no hour figure at
all**. So week 9's line — that it quotes no hour counts because only the
abstract was read — is exactly right, and can now be sharpened into a better
teaching point: the paper named after the question doesn't answer it in the
abstract, which is why the course doesn't either.

**New detail the week may add from this abstract (it currently uses none of
it):** the designs are **Study 1, N = 355 MTurk participants who had recently
relocated**, and **Study 2, N = 112 first-year students, three waves over 9
weeks**; the framing is **Dunbar's social brain hypothesis** and **Communicate
Bond Belong theory**; and **two types of everyday talk** predicted changes in
closeness (the abstract does not say which two — do not guess).

## C2. Sprecher, Treger & Wondra (2012) — listening beat disclosing

**Citation.** Sprecher, S., Treger, S., & Wondra, J. D. (2012). Effects of
self-disclosure role on liking, closeness, and other impressions in
get-acquainted interactions. *Journal of Social and Personal Relationships*,
30(4), 497–514.

**DOI.** `10.1177/0265407512459033` — 146 citations.

**Abstract (verbatim, Crossref).**

> We experimentally examined whether giving or receiving self-disclosure leads
> to greater liking and other positive impressions (e.g., closeness) in initial
> interactions. We also contributed to a recent debate about the
> familiarity-attraction link by examining whether knowledge about another leads
> to greater (or lesser) degrees of liking and perceived similarity. Pairs of
> unacquainted undergraduates completed a structured self-disclosure task. We
> randomly assigned one participant to disclose while the other listened in a
> first interaction; participants switched roles for a second interaction. After
> the first interaction, listeners (vs. disclosers) reported more liking and
> other positive interpersonal impressions. These differences disappeared after
> participants switched roles in the second interaction. Furthermore, listening
> was associated with greater degrees of perceived similarity.

**What the week could claim from this abstract alone.** This is the strongest
candidate in the brief. Week 9 already tells students "Listening is a move, not
a pause between moves" and attributes the idea to Collins & Miller's third
effect. Sprecher et al. is an **experiment** that puts a result behind it:
**after the first interaction, the listeners reported more liking than the
disclosers**, and **listening was associated with greater perceived
similarity**. And the sting in the tail is pure week 9: **the difference
disappeared once they swapped roles** — which is reciprocity doing its work,
and it is the depth scale's ±1 rule with evidence underneath it. One-way
disclosure leaves an asymmetry; taking a turn each removes it.

**Caveats to state:** unacquainted undergraduates, a **structured** disclosure
task in a lab, initial interactions only. It says nothing about a café queue,
and the course should say so — same caution it already applies to Aron.
