# Week 4 research brief --- candidates for "Latency and Turn-Taking"

Discovery only. Abstracts below are **verbatim** from the source named against
each one. Nothing here has been written into a lecture, deck or reading.

**Named gap.** The week's "timeout" model (by ~700 ms a no is more likely)
rests on a single paper, Kendrick & Torreira (2015). It needs (a) independent
evidence on what a silence is *heard as*, and (b) something on how gap length
actually varies rather than one central figure.

## C4.1 --- Roberts, Margutti & Takano (2011) — STRONGEST

**Citation.** Roberts, F., Margutti, P., & Takano, S. (2011). Judgments
concerning the valence of inter-turn silence across speakers of American
English, Italian, and Japanese. *Discourse Processes*, 48(5), 331–354.

**DOI.** `10.1080/0163853x.2011.558002` · Crossref cites: 74 · journal-article

**Abstract source.** OpenAlex, reconstructed from `abstract_inverted_index`.

> The fact that people with minimal linguistic skill can manage in unfamiliar
> or reduced linguistic environments suggests that there are universal
> mechanisms of meaning construction that operate at a level well beyond the
> particular structure or semantics of any one language. The authors examine
> this possibility in the domain of discourse by focusing on how gaps arising
> at the juncture between 2 persons' turns-at-talk (inter-turn silences) are
> evaluated by speakers of typologically distinct languages: English, Italian,
> and Japanese. This cross-linguistic design allows the testing of both
> universal and relative aspects in orientation to silence. For this study,
> the effects of inter-turn silence are tested using study participants'
> ratings of speakers' willingness to comply with requests or agree with
> assessments that were embedded in conversations. In a 3 × 2 × 3
> between-groups design, 3 silence lengths (0 ms, 600 ms, or 1200 ms) were
> crossed with 2 speech act types (requests and assessments) in manipulations
> of telephone conversations that were modeled on an actual telephone call
> between friends. Native-speaking study participants, in their home
> countries, provided ratings on Likert-type scales. Ratings significantly
> decreased within each language group at longer inter-turn silences,
> indicating a generalized response to the gaps; however, means were also
> significantly different between groups, indicating different expectations
> for agreement.

**What the week could claim from this abstract alone.** Longer inter-turn
silence lowered raters' judgements of a speaker's willingness to comply or
agree, and it did so *within every one of the three language groups* (0 ms,
600 ms, 1200 ms tested) — independent support for the timeout, from an
experiment rather than a corpus. And the group means differed significantly,
so the *expectation* a silence is read against is language-specific even though
the direction of the effect is not.

**Why it matters for the argument.** This is the week's second source for its
central claim, and it is a manipulation rather than an observation, so it gets
at the listener's inference directly. It also hands the week a genuinely new
point: the inference is general, the calibration is local. Note it tests
telephone conversations modelled on a real call between friends.

## C4.2 --- Heldner & Edlund (2010) — PARTIAL, abstract truncated at source

**Citation.** Heldner, M., & Edlund, J. (2010). Pauses, gaps and overlaps in
conversations. *Journal of Phonetics*, 38(4), 555–568.

**DOI.** `10.1016/j.wocn.2010.08.002` · Crossref cites: 321 (Semantic Scholar: 593)

**Abstract source.** Semantic Scholar. **Crossref and OpenAlex have no
abstract at all.** The Semantic Scholar record is itself truncated — it is 201
characters and ends in an ellipsis. Verbatim, in full, as stored:

> This paper explores durational aspects of pauses gaps and overlaps in three
> different conversational corpora with a view to challenge claims about
> precision timing in turn-taking Distributions of p ...

**What the week could claim from this abstract alone.** Only that the paper
examines durations of pauses, gaps and overlaps across three conversational
corpora, and that it sets out to *challenge* claims about precision timing in
turn-taking. **No number from this paper can be used**, and the nature of the
challenge is cut off mid-sentence.

**Recommendation.** Either cite it narrowly for the existence of a challenge
to precision-timing claims — which would actually suit the week's errata
entry E-1, "not a hard number" — or cut it. Do not quote a distribution from
it without the full text.

## C4.3 --- Roberts, Francis & Morgan (2006) — UNVERIFIED

interaction of inter-turn silence with prosodic cues in listener perceptions
of "trouble" in conversation. *Speech Communication*, 48(9), 1079–1093.

**Abstract source.** **None found.** Crossref, OpenAlex and Semantic Scholar

**Status: UNVERIFIED.** The title implies exactly what the week wants — inter-turn
silence interacting with prosody in how listeners perceive "trouble" — which
is precisely why it should not be cited on the strength of its title. This was
already a cut lead in the original plan; nothing found here changes that.
Recommend leaving cut unless someone reads the full text.

## Note: a week 5 candidate that bears directly on week 4

`C5.3` in `week-05.md` — Kendrick (2015), *Frontiers in Psychology* — has a
full verified abstract that opens with turn transitions occurring "with only a
slight gap of ∼100–300 ms between them" and states that this central tendency
"hides a wealth of complex variation," with the type of turn involved shown to
influence transition timing. That is the variation evidence this week's gap
(b) asked for, and it is verified where C4.2 is not. Worth considering for
week 4 as well as week 5.
