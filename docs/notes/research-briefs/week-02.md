## VERIFIED --- 1. Nisbett & Wilson (1977)

**Citation.** Nisbett, R. E., & Wilson, T. D. (1977). Telling more than we can
know: Verbal reports on mental processes. *Psychological Review*, 84(3),
231--259.

**DOI.** `10.1037/0033-295X.84.3.231`

**Cited by.** 7,206 (Crossref) / 11,369 (OpenAlex).

**Abstract (verbatim, from OpenAlex, reconstructed from the publisher's
inverted index).**

> Evidence is reviewed which suggests that there may be little or no direct
> introspective access to higher order cognitive processes. Subjects are
> sometimes (a) unaware of the existence of a stimulus that importantly
> influenced a response, (b) unaware of the existence of the response, and (c)
> unaware that the stimulus has affected the response. It is proposed that when
> people attempt to report on their cognitive processes, that is, on the
> processes mediating the effects of a stimulus on a response, they do not do so
> on the basis of any true introspection. Instead, their reports are based on a
> priori, implicit causal theories, or judgments about the extent to which a
> particular stimulus is a plausible cause of a given response. This suggests
> that though people may not be able to observe directly their cognitive
> processes, they will sometimes be able to report accurately about them.
> Accurate reports will occur when influential stimuli are salient and are
> plausible causes of the responses they produce, and will not occur when
> stimuli are not salient or are not plausible causes.

**What the week could claim from this.** That a fluent user's inability to
state the protocol's rules is not evasion or stupidity: the review argues there
may be little or no direct introspective access to higher-order cognitive
processes, and that self-reports are built from implicit causal theories rather
than observation. Note the abstract's own hedge, which the course should keep:
reports are sometimes accurate, specifically when the influential stimulus is
salient and a plausible cause.

**Caution.** This is a review of evidence about cognitive processes generally,
not about social conventions or about autism. It supports "people are poor
witnesses to their own processing"; it does not by itself support any claim
about greeting rules.

## VERIFIED --- 2. Heasman & Gillespie (2018)

**Citation.** Heasman, B., & Gillespie, A. (2018). Perspective-taking is
two-sided: Misunderstandings between people with Asperger's syndrome and their
family members. *Autism*, 22(6), 740--750.

**DOI.** `10.1177/1362361317708287`

**Cited by.** 144 (Crossref) / 163 (OpenAlex).

**Year note.** Crossref's `issued` field says 2017 (online-first); the issue is
22(6), 2018. Pick one deliberately and use it consistently.

**Abstract (verbatim, from OpenAlex, reconstructed from the publisher's
inverted index).**

> Misunderstandings are social in nature, always having two sides. Yet the
> misunderstandings experienced by people with Asperger's syndrome are usually
> studied in terms of the individual with a diagnosis, with less emphasis on
> social relations. We use a two-sided methodology to map out misunderstandings
> within 22 dyads (n = 44) consisting of people with Asperger's syndrome and
> their family members. Both sides of the relationship were asked about 12
> topics in terms of one's rating of Self, one's rating of Other and one's
> predicted rating by Other. The findings show that people with Asperger's are
> able to predict lower scores from family members, despite disagreeing with
> their view, and that family members often over-estimate the extent to which
> their relatives with Asperger's syndrome are egocentrically anchored in their
> own perspective. The research demonstrates that a two-sided methodology is
> viable, and it uses it to identify how representations of Asperger's syndrome
> can both support and hinder social understanding within relationships
> affected by Asperger's.

**What the week could claim from this.** That the double empathy problem has
been measured from both sides in real relationships, and that the error is
asymmetric in a specific, nameable way: the autistic party could predict the
family member's lower rating while disagreeing with it, and the family members
over-estimated how egocentrically anchored their relative was. This is a much
stronger mechanism than Garfinkel's breaching alone gives, and it puts a
measured error on the majority side.

**Caution.** The paper's own vocabulary is "Asperger's syndrome", a clinical
term the course does not use in its own voice. Any quotation of the construct
needs `data-quoted`. Sample is 22 dyads recruited around a diagnosis; this is
not a population estimate.

## VERIFIED --- 3. Alkhaldi, Sheppard & Mitchell (2019)

**Citation.** Alkhaldi, R. S., Sheppard, E., & Mitchell, P. (2019). Is there a
link between autistic people being perceived unfavorably and having a mind that
is difficult to read? *Journal of Autism and Developmental Disorders*, 49(10),
3973--3982.

**DOI.** `10.1007/s10803-019-04101-1`

**Cited by.** 66 (Crossref) / 83 (OpenAlex).

**Abstract (verbatim, from OpenAlex, reconstructed from the publisher's
inverted index).**

> The link between autistic people having a mind that is difficult to read (by
> neurotypical participants) and being perceived unfavorably was investigated.
> Videoed Autistic and neurotypical targets from Sheppard et al. (PLOS ONE
> 7(11):e49859, 2016) were scored for how readable they were when reacting to a
> distinctive greeting from the experimenter. These videos were presented to new
> groups of perceivers (neurotypical adults) who rated neurotypical targets more
> socially favorably than autistic targets irrespective of whether details of
> the experimenter's greeting were concealed (Study 1) or disclosed (Study 2).
> Target readability correlated with ratings of target favorability (r = .58 and
> r = .63), independent of target diagnosis. Perceivers might rate targets
> unfavorably because they experience difficulty reading them, though other
> interpretations of the correlation are also possible.

**What the week could claim from this.** That week 2's Sasson finding has a
candidate mechanism with a number on it: how *readable* a person was correlated
with how favourably they were rated (r = .58, r = .63), and that correlation
held **independent of diagnosis** --- so it is about readability, not about
being autistic. Note that this study reuses the stimulus videos from the
Sheppard work week 11 already cites, which ties the two weeks together.

**Caution.** The authors explicitly decline the causal claim: "Perceivers
*might* rate targets unfavorably because they experience difficulty reading
them, though other interpretations of the correlation are also possible." The
course must not upgrade this to cause. Also note the stimulus is a reaction to
"a distinctive greeting from the experimenter", which is a nice link to week 1
but is a contrived greeting, not a corridor one.

## UNVERIFIED --- 4. Reber (1989)

**Citation.** Reber, A. S. (1989). Implicit learning and tacit knowledge.
*Journal of Experimental Psychology: General*, 118(3), 219--235.

**DOI.** `10.1037/0096-3445.118.3.219`

**Cited by.** 1,359 (Crossref) / 1,585 (OpenAlex).

**Abstract.** **Not available.** Checked Crossref, OpenAlex and Semantic
Scholar; all three return no abstract. Reber (1967), *Journal of Verbal
Learning and Verbal Behavior*, 6(6), 855--863, DOI
`10.1016/S0022-5371(67)80149-X`, is the same problem: no abstract anywhere.

**Status.** Do not cite for any specific finding. It is the obvious canonical
reference for "people acquire rule-governed knowledge they cannot state", and
the course would want exactly that claim --- but on the harness's rule, an
unverifiable abstract means the claim gets cut or restated as the course's own
observation. Nisbett & Wilson above covers adjacent ground and *is* verified,
so the recommendation is to use that instead and drop Reber.

## Summary for the parent

Three verified, and they line up unusually well: Nisbett & Wilson give the week
its missing mechanism for why fluent users can't describe the protocol;
Heasman & Gillespie put a measured, two-sided error into the double empathy
claim; Alkhaldi et al. supply a correlation behind the Sasson finding and reuse
the same stimulus videos as week 11's Sheppard citation.

Two cut for lack of any abstract (Reber, Emerson et al.) --- both are the
"obvious" citation for their point, which is exactly when the temptation to
cite from memory is strongest.
