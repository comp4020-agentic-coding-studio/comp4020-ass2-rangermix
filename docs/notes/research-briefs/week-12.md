## C12-1 --- Khaw & Vernon (2025) --- OI-1, and it reframes the issue

**Citation.** Khaw, J., & Vernon, T. (2025). Relationship Satisfaction Among
Autistic Populations: How Partner Neurotype Influences Relationship
Satisfaction Factors for Autistic Adults. *Autism in Adulthood*, 8(4),
720--734.

**DOI.** `10.1089/aut.2024.0124`

**Abstract (verbatim, Crossref --- scientific abstract only; the record also
carries a long "Community Brief" which I have not reproduced).**

> Background: Autistic individuals report high levels of interest in pursuing
> and maintaining romantic relationships but indicate fewer and less satisfying
> relationships when compared with non-autistic populations. In this
> mixed-methods study, we investigated the self-identified factors that
> contribute to successful romantic relationships for autistic adults with the
> additional goal of understanding if and how their partners' neurotypes may
> influence relationship satisfaction. Methods: We compared levels of
> relationship satisfaction among 106 autistic individuals by categorizing each
> participant into one of three groups based on the specific neurotype of their
> partner: (1) autistic/autistic (A/A) dyads; (2) autistic/neurotypical (A/NT)
> dyads; and (3) autistic/non-autistic but neurodivergent (A/ND) dyads. All
> participants completed a series of online surveys to gather information about
> their autistic traits, overall relationship satisfaction, reciprocal
> communication and support, and perceived impact of autism on relationships.
> Results: Across the three dyads, participants reported similar levels of
> relationship satisfaction, and we found no statistically significant
> differences when comparing the mean satisfaction of each sample group.
> Participants indicated a wide variety of positive and negative relational
> aspects, with a few themes changing in frequency based on partner neurotype.
> Participants with autistic partners tended to focus on an inherent and deep
> sense of understanding when discussing the positive aspects of their
> relationships, while those with non-autistic partners were more likely to
> discuss the presence of mutual support and accommodation. In addition to some
> differences in reported themes, participants tended to report that their
> autistic traits positively impacted satisfaction in A/A dyads, while those in
> A/NT and A/ND dyads tended to report that their autistic traits posed
> challenges in their relationships. Conclusions: The results of this study
> indicate that the neurotype of an autistic individual's partner may influence
> some of the reported positive and challenging aspects of their relationships.
> However, survey results indicate that autistic populations generally find
> comparable levels of satisfaction in romantic relationships irrespective of
> neurotype, which suggests that while partner neurotype may shape the enabling
> and barring factors for relationship success, it does not directly affect the
> overall health of a relationship.

**What the week could claim from this abstract alone.** Satisfaction was
**comparable across all three partner-neurotype pairings** — so partner
neurotype shaped *what was named as good or hard* without changing overall
satisfaction. The thematic split is the useful teaching detail: autistic
partners were described in terms of **an inherent and deep sense of
understanding**; non-autistic partners in terms of **mutual support and
accommodation**. That maps exactly onto week 11's conversion/interoperation
distinction, arriving in OI-1 rather than being asserted there.

**Caution.** n = 106, self-report, partner neurotype not verified by the
researchers (their own stated limitation), few male participants. The
background sentence repeats the "fewer and less satisfying relationships"
comparison — the week should not restate that as its own claim, since this
study's own result is *no* satisfaction difference by pairing.

## C12-2 --- Ciric et al. (2025) --- OI-1, qualitative, and on-argument

**Citation.** Ciric, T., White, L. C. J., Allison-Duncan, C., Maloney, E., &
Gillespie-Smith, K. (2025). 'It's quite difficult to put Autistic
relationships in a box': A qualitative exploration of romantic relationships in
gender and sexually diverse Autistic adults. *Autism*, 30(3), 682--694.

**DOI.** `10.1177/13623613251407765`

**Abstract (verbatim, Crossref).**

> Autistic people enjoy friendships and sexual and romantic relationships. Many
> Autistic people are also part of a gender or sexual minority, yet gender and
> sexual minorities are often under-represented in autism research. The current
> study asked, 'How do gender and sexually diverse Autistic adults experience
> and perceive romantic relationships?' Sixteen gender and/or sexual minority
> Autistic adults took part in semi-structured interviews. Interviews were
> transcribed and analysed using thematic analysis. Four main themes were
> identified from the interview data: (1) 'It's Quite Difficult to Put Autistic
> Relationships Into a Box', (2) Challenging Social Norms, (3) The Perks and
> Perils of Online Dating and (4) Understanding and Neurotype. Overall,
> participants described their relationships as individual, inherently valuable
> and shaped by unique preferences, mutual understanding and explicit
> communication. They also emphasized the fluidity between friendships and
> romantic partnerships, the importance of mutual understanding and the
> inherent value of rejecting prescriptive social norms in favour of
> individualized romantic relationship dynamics.

**What the week could claim from this abstract alone.** Participants described
relationships shaped by **explicit communication** and by **rejecting
prescriptive social norms in favour of individualized dynamics** — which is
the course's whole thesis showing up in the one area it declared out of scope.
Note the Crossref record gives year 2025 but volume 30(3), which reads like a
2026 issue; **the parent should confirm year/volume before citing.**

**Caution.** n = 16, interviews, a specific population (gender and/or sexual
minority autistic adults) — not generalisable to autistic adults' dating
broadly, and the week must not present it as such.

## C12-3 --- Sturm & Antonakis (2014) --- OI-3, the closest thing found to the critique

**Citation.** Sturm, R. E., & Antonakis, J. (2014). Interpersonal Power: A
Review, Critique, and Research Agenda. *Journal of Management*, 41(1),
136--163. (Crossref gives the title simply as "Interpersonal Power"; the
subtitle appears in the published article. Parent should confirm the exact
form before citing.)

**DOI.** `10.1177/0149206314555769`

**Abstract (verbatim, Crossref).**

> Power is a fundamental force in social relationships and is pervasive
> throughout various types of interactions. Although research has shown that
> the possession of power can change the power holder, the full extent of
> power's consequences on individuals' decision-making capabilities and social
> interactions within organizations is not fully understood. The goal of this
> paper is to review, synthesize, and critique the literature on power with a
> focus on its organizational and managerial implications. Specifically, we
> propose a definition of power that takes into account its three defining
> characteristics—having the discretion and means to enforce one's will—and
> summarize the extant literature on how power influences individuals'
> thoughts, emotions, and actions both in terms of prosocial and antisocial
> outcomes. In addition, we highlight important moderators of power and
> describe ways in which it can be studied in a more rigorous manner by
> examining methodological issues and pitfalls with regard to its measurement
> and manipulation. We also provide future research directions to motivate and
> guide the study of power by management scholars.

**What the week could claim from this abstract alone.** That the power
literature has been **reviewed and critiqued on methodological grounds**, with
specific attention to **pitfalls in how power is measured and manipulated**,
and that power's effects have **important moderators**. That supports OI-3's
"the research is still arguing" and "treat it as a proposal, not a settled
result".

**Important limitation — read before using.** This abstract does **not** name
Keltner, Gruenfeld & Anderson, and does **not** mention the
approach/inhibition account. It is a critique of the power literature's
methods in general, in a *management* journal. So it **cannot** be cited as
"the criticism this specific account drew". The honest options are:
(a) cite it for the narrower, true claim that power's measurement and
manipulation have been criticised methodologically; or (b) leave OI-3's
"published criticism" line uncited and restate it as the course's own caution,
per the harness rule. **I did not find a direct published critique or failed
replication of the approach/inhibition theory.** Searches on
replication/critique terms returned critical-theory and unrelated results.

One lead not pursued: Wei (2025), "The behavioral theory of approach-inhibition
of power: Theoretical..." in *Advances in Psychological Science* 33(2), 274,
DOI `10.3724/sp.j.1042.2025.0274`. **UNVERIFIED** — no abstract in Crossref, 1
citation, and the journal publishes in Chinese, so the parent would need to
confirm it is readable and peer-reviewed before relying on it. Its title
suggests it is a theoretical review of exactly the account OI-3 describes.
