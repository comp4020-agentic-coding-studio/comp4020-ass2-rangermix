## Three time scales

| State | Scale | From inside |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Overload** | this afternoon | input arriving faster than it can be processed |
| **Shutdown** | this evening | output stops; words are hard to find |
| **Burnout** | this semester | overspent for weeks, and rest isn't refilling it |

The distinctions are the course's. Only the third has a research definition.

```notes
- These are planning time scales, not recovery deadlines. The table distinguishes immediate load from load that has accumulated for weeks.
```

## Where that table is wrong

The literature names **four** phenomena, not three. **BIMS**:

**b**urnout · **i**nertia · **m**eltdown · **s**hutdown

Two of those --- inertia and meltdown --- **are not in the course's table at all.**

<span class="source">Phung, Penner, Pirlot & Welch (2021), Frontiers in Psychology 12, 741421</span>

<MarginNote label="Errata, early">Two named states are missing from this map.</MarginNote>

```notes
- Do this correction here, not in week 12's errata. They need it before they write a runbook.
- Leave room in the runbook for states beyond the course's three rows.
```

## What BIMS looks like from inside

Eight autistic children and youth, interviewed one-to-one with augmentative communication and visual supports allowed.

BIMS described as a **multi-faceted experience** --- **emotional, cognitive and physical** components at once.

And **often misunderstood** by the people around them.

<span class="source">Phung et al. (2021)</span>

```notes
- Eight young people, and not adults. Say both limits out loud; no prevalence claim comes out of this.
- Use the emotional, cognitive and physical components as prompts; do not present the three course rows as exhaustive.
```

## Two studies, one definition

Autistic adults discussed autistic burnout among themselves for years while the academic and clinical literature barely mentioned it.

Then two studies defined it **with** the people who experience it.

```notes
- Keep the sequence clear: community descriptions preceded these research definitions.
```

## Defined by the people who have it

Autistic adults discussed autistic burnout among themselves while the academic and clinical literature barely mentioned it.

**Chronic exhaustion, loss of skills, reduced tolerance to stimulus** --- from **cumulative load** and **barriers to relief**.

<span class="source">Raymaker et al. (2020), Autism in Adulthood 2(2)</span>

```notes
- Name the community-based participatory approach when introducing the definition.
```

---

## And agreed by consensus

23 autistic adults, three survey rounds: **exhaustion, withdrawal, difficulties with executive function, generally reduced functioning, more pronounced autistic traits**.

<span class="source">Higgins et al. (2021), Autism 25(8)</span>

Both studies: distinct from depression and from ordinary work burnout. Both name masking and an unaccommodating environment among the causes.

```notes
- That's the thread straight from week 6. The ledger's costs accumulate.
```

---

{/* _class: impact */}

The way out is removing load.

Not adding rest on top of it.

<MarginNote label="Incident review">The fix recommended by the people who have it. Not "try harder at resting".</MarginNote>

```notes
- Raymaker's participants linked recovery to acceptance and support, time off or reduced expectations, and doing things in an autistic way, including unmasking.
- Their authors note the danger of teaching autistic people to mask.
```

## Overload is measurable, not just describable

98 Swedish adults --- **62 autistic, 36 non-autistic** --- on sensory experience and communication in **health care settings**.

Autistic participants reported **greater discomfort with background sound levels**, and felt **more misunderstood by providers**.

<span class="source">Strömberg, Liman, Bang & Igelström (2022), Autism in Adulthood 4(1)</span>

```notes
- This is a comparison of reported discomfort in Swedish health care settings; do not imply that the questionnaire observed everyone in the same room.
- And note the setting. It is one this lecture is about to tell them to contact. Park that; it comes back at the end.
```

## In the room: name your own early signs (6 min)

Two minutes, alone, no discussion: the **first three things** you notice when load is climbing.

Not what you feel --- what you **do**, or stop doing. One emotional, one cognitive, one physical.

Four minutes in pairs: read only your **first** sign, and have your partner say whether anyone would notice it from outside.

```notes
- If a sign would be hard for others to notice, ask what the student could tell them when it appears. Do not assume the room will share one answer.
```

## Recovery, for everyone in the room

Four kinds of **recovery experience**, separated in occupational health research:

**Detachment** · **Relaxation** · **Mastery** · **Control**

<span class="source">Sonnentag & Fritz (2007), Journal of Occupational Health Psychology 12(3)</span>

```notes
- Their research is employees recovering from ordinary work stress. The course does not claim it treats or prevents burnout.
- Use the four experiences as a planning checklist. One slide each.
```

---

## Detachment

**Not thinking about the work.**

Not "thinking about it more calmly". Not thinking about it.

```notes
- Ask which phone notifications bring work back into the break, and whether any could be turned off.
```

---

## Relaxation

**Low activation, low effort.**

The one people mean when they say rest --- and only one of four.

---

## Mastery

**Something challenging that isn't the work.**

A skill, a craft, a game you get better at. Demanding, and nothing to do with the degree.

```notes
- Counter-intuitive, and worth dwelling on: effort can be recovery if it is the right kind of effort.
```

---

## Control

**Choosing what you do with the time.**

Which is why a fortnight of obligations is not a break, however little work is in it.

```notes
- A family visit can fill a calendar with obligations even without coursework. Use it as an example of limited control over time.
```

## Why a runbook and not a resolution

Trying to decide what to drop **while** you are struggling adds another decision to the load.

A runbook records those decisions **in advance**, when you have more capacity.

```notes
- Point forward to the six sections: each removes a decision from the difficult day.
```

---

## Six sections

| Section | What goes in it |
| --- | --- |
| Early signs | what you notice first, in your own words |
| First three actions | things that need no decisions |
| Messages | pre-written, saved, ready to send |
| Who gets told | names, and what each needs to know |
| **What gets dropped** | load to remove, in order |
| Recovery | what refills, from past experience |

Six sections. Write it this week, while writing is cheap.

```notes
- "I stop replying to the group chat. Loud rooms get louder." That's an early-signs line in someone's own words.
```

---

## Early signs

What you notice **first**, in your own words.

> "I stop replying to the group chat. Loud rooms get louder."

Yours, not a checklist. The exercise you just did is this row.

---

## First three actions

Things that need **no decisions**, because deciding is what runs out.

> "Headphones. Eat something. Cancel the optional thing."

```notes
- Three, in order, and no branching. If a step needs a judgement call it belongs in a different section.
```

---

## Who gets told

Names, and **what each person needs to know** --- which is different for each.

> "Housemate: need quiet. Tutor: may miss lab."

```notes
- The point is that the housemate does not need the tutor's information and vice versa. Week 10 is this idea at full size.
```

---

## Recovery

What refills, **from past experience** rather than from what ought to work.

> "A day with no plans. The long walk. The game."

---

## What gets dropped

Fill in **what gets dropped** before adding recovery activities.

> 1. Social plans. 2. Non-assessed reading. 3. Replying within a day.

A runbook that only adds recovery activities to a full week hasn't changed the load.

```notes
- Raymaker's participants and Higgins's both point at removing load. Adding rest on top of the same load is the mistake this row prevents.
```

- Link this instruction to participants' reports of time off and reduced expectations.

## In the room: draft one message (8 min)

Pick **one** of the three. Write it now, on your phone, in the notes app you actually use.

Constraints: under 40 words; no apology longer than one clause; and it must **pre-empt "are you okay?"** so the reply costs them nothing.

Then read it back and delete one sentence.

```notes
- On the phone, not on paper. A message in a notebook is not saved where it will be needed.
- The deletion tests which information the recipient needs. Ask what changed when that sentence came out.
```

---

## Three messages, saved now

> **To a tutor.** Subject: [NT101] Lab: absent Thursday. "I won't be at Thursday's lab. I'll work from the lab page and email by Monday if I need anything. No reply needed."

> **To a group chat.** "I'm going quiet for a few days. Nothing's wrong between us. I'll message when I'm back."

> **To someone you live with.** "Rough day. I need the room quiet tonight. It's not about you."

```notes
- Composing a message costs exactly what runs out first. Two taps, no editing.
- Each one pre-empts "are you okay?", which would take another message to answer.
```

## One honest word about the services

Strömberg et al. found autistic adults reporting more sensory discomfort, and feeling more misunderstood, in health care settings than non-autistic adults did.

That was Swedish health care, not this university.

It is **not** a reason to avoid asking for help. It is a reason to plan the ask while you can --- which is what **messages** and **who gets told** are for.

```notes
- Do not let this slide become a reason not to seek help. Say the last line twice if you have to.
```

## When it's more than a runbook

Both burnout studies report that it can bring thoughts of suicide.

A runbook is not care, and this part isn't a protocol.

If you're thinking about suicide, or you're not safe, **tell someone today**.

- **Lifeline: 13 11 14**, or text **0477 13 11 14**, any time
- **Emergency: 000**
- The policies page lists the university's support services

Your tutor and convenor will help you reach them.

```notes
- Slow right down here. No deadpan, no protocol vocabulary, no joke anywhere near this slide.
- Say the last line as itself: getting help to people who ask is what those services are for.
```
