## The speaker gets first go

Conversation has built-in machinery for trouble with speaking, hearing and understanding: the **organisation of repair**.

A strong skew inside it: speakers fix their own trouble far more often than anyone else fixes it for them.

<span class="source">Schegloff, Jefferson & Sacks (1977), Language 53(2)</span>

```notes
- So "Meet you Thursday, I mean next Thursday" happens before a listener has to say anything.
```

---

## Once every 1.4 minutes

48.5 hours of conversation, twelve languages, eight language families.

**2053** cases of a listener signalling trouble --- about one every **1.4 minutes**, in every language sampled.

<span class="source">Dingemanse et al. (2015), PLOS ONE 10(9)</span>

<MarginNote label="Uptime">If your conversations have gone ninety seconds without a repair, the logs are not complete.</MarginNote>

## Three ways to say you didn't get it

| Type | What it does | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Open request | flags trouble, not where | "Huh?" / "Sorry, what?" |
| Restricted request | asks about one part | "Who?" / "Which Thursday?" |
| Restricted offer | offers a guess | "This Thursday?" |

Every language sampled had all three --- and people choose the **most specific initiator available**.

<span class="source">Dingemanse et al. (2015), PLOS ONE 10(9)</span>

```notes
- Most specific costs both sides least. A restricted offer means the speaker says one word; an open request means they repeat everything.
```

---

## So: say the part you got

> "Sorry, you want the draft *by* Friday, or *on* Friday?"

Shows the speaker exactly which part you caught.

```notes
- Have students say the example aloud.
```

## When repair begins

169 other-initiations of repair, timed. Those that **located the trouble specifically** arrived after **shorter gaps** than those that didn't.

The rare ones that simply **corrected an error** arrived **without delay**.

The most frequent gap before a repair initiation was about **700 ms**.

<span class="source">Kendrick (2015), Frontiers in Psychology 6, 250</span>

```notes
- The 700 ms is the same figure as week 4's timeout, reached from a different direction. Point at it.
- The move that flags trouble is itself one of the slow turns. Worth saying to anyone who has felt too late to ask.
```

---

{/* _class: impact */}

Part of what that delay is for: giving the first speaker a chance to fix it themselves.

```notes
- Silence before a repair is not only hesitation. Sometimes it is room.
```

## "Huh?" is a word

Found in much the same form and function across ten languages --- far more alike than chance would explain.

Learnt, not an involuntary noise. It exists because every language needs a way to say *I didn't get that*.

<span class="source">Dingemanse, Torreira & Enfield (2013), PLoS ONE 8(11)</span>

```notes
- "Sorry, what?" is a repair request.
- The "sorry" is optional. Say that line flatly and move on.
```

## Accept in one turn

"Got it." / "Ah, next Thursday."

Apologising for a misunderstanding --- from either side --- turns a one-turn repair into a three-turn one.

And it moves the conversation's attention from Thursday to the two of you.

---

## In the room: the repair drill (8 min)

In pairs. One reads a sentence from the board with **one word deliberately mumbled**.

The other must repair it using the **most specific initiator available** --- no bare "what?" allowed.

Six rounds, swapping. Count how many took one turn to fix.

```notes
- The no-bare-what rule is the whole exercise. It forces them to name the part they got, which is the skill.
- Debrief on the ones that took more than one turn. Did the mumble's position make a difference?
```

---

## Why the skew matters

Speakers fix their own trouble far more often than listeners fix it for them --- and that is a *design* property, not politeness.

Leaving a gap can give the speaker time to repair the problem without a prompt.

```notes
- Compare the three findings: who repairs the trouble, how long the gap is, and what the gap can allow.
```

## The cost table

| Initiator | What the speaker must do | Turns to fix |
| --- | --- | --- |
| "Huh?" | repeat the whole thing | 2, often 3 |
| "Which Thursday?" | supply one word | 2 |
| "This Thursday?" | say yes, or one word | 2, sometimes 1 |
| (silence, 700 ms) | notice and self-repair | 1 |

The course's proposed cost table. Test the turn counts in the repair drill.

```notes
- This is the argument for specificity in one shape: every step down the table costs the other person less.
- The last row needs no spoken repair request; it still depends on the speaker noticing the trouble.
```

## When the error is an offence

Mishearing a day is cheap. Letting someone down isn't, and the protocol switches subroutines.

Six apology components tested, alone and combined: **apologies with more components were rated more effective**, and some counted for more than others.

<span class="source">Lewicki, Polin & Lount (2016), Negotiation and Conflict Management Research 9(2)</span>

```notes
- We read the abstract, not the full paper, so the course won't claim which component matters most.
```

---

## Same direction, plus a mechanism

Two experimental vignette studies: more verbal elements increased forgiveness, **especially after a more severe offence**.

And the link between completeness and forgiveness was **mediated by anger reduction**.

<span class="source">Kirchhoff, Wagner & Strack (2012), Peace and Conflict 18(2)</span>

```notes
- Keep the claim tied to the vignette scenarios and the reported role of anger reduction.
```

---

## The four that mattered

For a more severe offence, Study 2 found these particularly relevant:

- conveying **emotions**
- **admitting fault**
- a **statement of apology** --- "I apologize"
- an attempt at **explanation**

<span class="source">Kirchhoff, Wagner & Strack (2012)</span>

```notes
- Read the fourth one twice, then stop.
```

## The template, corrected

| Part | Sounds like |
| --- | --- |
| **Say it** | "I'm sorry." |
| Name what happened | "I didn't send my section before the meeting." |
| Say it was yours | "That's on me, not the deadline." |
| Say what it cost them | "You had to present without it." |
| **Explain, briefly** | "I'd misread the day." |
| Say what happens now | "It's in the doc now, and I'll send the next one early." |
| Offer a fix | "Want me to take the Q&A next time?" |

Two rows are new.

```notes
- "I'm sorry" was missing entirely, which is faintly absurd for an apology template.
- The explanation is now in, with two constraints that are the course's own.
```

---

## Where the explanation goes

**After** the ownership, never before.

**Shorter** than the apology.

An explanation that arrives first, or runs long, can be heard as an excuse.

---

## The one thing still refused

> "but"

Everything before it gets deleted by everything after it.

```notes
- And the caveat, said out loud: Kirchhoff is two vignette studies in Germany. The direction is well supported; the exact four are not a universal parts list.
```

---

## And the part still easiest to leave out

**What it cost them.**

Without it, an apology is about you. With it, they know you saw it from their side.

That row is the course's, not a finding.

---

## Repair is not a personality

Meta-analysis: **175 studies, 26,006 participants**, on whether one person forgives another.

- apology **r = .42**
- state anger **r = −.41**

<span class="source">Fehr, Gelfand & Nag (2010), Psychological Bulletin 136(5)</span>

```notes
- The anger correlate sits beside Kirchhoff's anger-reduction route, from a completely different direction.
```

---

{/* _class: impact */}

Situational constructs accounted for greater variance in forgiveness than victims' dispositions.

<MarginNote label="Closing the loop">The situation belongs in the bug report.</MarginNote>

```notes
- Use the comparison as a reason to examine the situation, not to rank causes for a particular apology.
- Separate the association from a guarantee: an apology can matter without ensuring forgiveness.
```

## Two folk theories, same abstract

Effect of **gender: non-significant** (r = .01).

Effect of **age: negligible** (r = .06).

<span class="source">Fehr, Gelfand & Nag (2010)</span>

## In the room: write the hard one (10 min)

On your own, four minutes: write a real apology you owe, or owed, using the corrected template. Every row. Nobody reads it out.

Then, in pairs, six minutes: swap only the **"what it cost them"** line, and say whether your partner's line is about them or about the writer.

```notes
- Say clearly that only the cost line is shared; nobody reads the whole apology out.
- The cost line is the only part swapped because it's the part that decides whether the apology is about them.
```

## Receiving it

- Someone repairs a misunderstanding of yours: **update and carry on.** "Oh, next Thursday. Great." Nothing went wrong.
- Someone apologises: **accept in one turn**, if you can mean it. "Thanks for saying that. We're fine."
- If you can't mean it yet: "Thanks. I need a bit of time" is conforming.

```notes
- Say both acceptance scripts aloud, including the option to ask for time.
```
